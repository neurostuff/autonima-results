Effects of attentional bias modification therapy on the cue reactivity and cognitive control networks in participants with cocaine use disorders

ABSTRACT

Background

While attentional bias modification therapy (ABMT) alters drug-related behaviors in some substance users, results have been mixed in individuals with cocaine use disorders (CUD).

Objectives

The current study examined whether ABMT affected brain functioning during independent measures of cue reactivity (i.e., cocaine versus food cues) and cognitive control (i.e., incongruent versus congruent trials), and whether brain activity was associated with baseline or post-intervention cocaine use.

Methods

37 participants (62% male) were randomly assigned to ABMT or control therapy. Clinical and neuroimaging assessments occurred at baseline and immediately post-intervention, with additional clinical testing at 2 weeks and 3 months following intervention. Cocaine use was assessed through self-report.

Results

Slower reaction times and increased functional activation (prefrontal cortex, posterior parietal cortex) were observed for incongruent versus congruent stimuli and increased functional activation for cocaine relative to food videos (ventral striatum, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and orbitofrontal cortex). The default-mode network (DMN) was not deactivated during exposure to cocaine videos. The degree of activation during cocaine relative to food cues was associated with baseline cocaine use (insula only) and reduction in use following treatment (insula and anterior DMN) above and beyond clinical variables. Cognitive control network activity was not associated with cocaine use at baseline or following treatment. ABMT therapy did not differentially affect cocaine use or functional activation during either task.

Conclusion

Current results suggest a relationship between cue reactivity network activation and cocaine use, but question the efficacy of ABMT in changing brain function during cue reactivity or cognitive control tasks.

Introduction

With repeated drug use, individuals become gradually sensitized to drugs and their associated stimuli (Citation1), resulting in an attentional bias for drug-related cues relative to other appetitive stimuli (Citation2–Citation5). This results in an abnormal functional organization of the addicted brain characterized by both an enhanced neural response for drug-related cues (i.e., cue reactivity) and abnormal activity within the cognitive control network (Citation6). The degree of attentional bias in alcohol and cannabis use disorders has been shown to be associated with treatment outcome in early studies (Citation7,Citation8). However, current findings in participants with CUD are mixed, showing either a negative association between drug salience and treatment outcome (Citation7) or no association (Citation9,Citation10). To our knowledge, no studies have investigated how attempting to alter attentional bias in participants with CUD may affect neural activity during cue reactivity (e.g., exposure to drug stimuli) or cognitive control (e.g., Stroop, flanker) tasks.

Attentional bias is a nonspecific term that has been previously used to describe faster (i.e., facilitation) or slower (i.e., interference) reaction times for drug relative to neutral stimuli (Citation10), but can also be measured with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Specifically, participants with CUD exhibit greater activation in ventral striatum, orbital frontal cortex (OFC), and lateral (lPFC) and dorsomedial (dmPFC) prefrontal cortex for drug-related stimuli relative to other appetitive cues when compared to non-using individuals (Citation11,Citation12), which has been posited to be a result of increased drug salience and a subsequent bias toward drug stimuli. Similarly, decreased activation within the dmPFC, lPFC and posterior parietal cortex during cognitive control tasks has been observed in participants with CUD (Citation13,Citation14), although hyper-activation of these structures has also been reported (Citation15). Finally, functional activity during cue reactivity (Citation11,Citation16,Citation17), cognitive control (Citation18,Citation19) and decision making (Citation20) tasks have all been associated with cocaine use and treatment outcomes. However, no studies have directly compared the ability of cue reactivity and cognitive control tasks to predict either baseline cocaine use or a reduction in use in the same cohort of subjects.

Attentional bias modification training (ABMT) attempts to involuntarily shift bias away from drug cues as a mechanism for reducing use, typically by having probes replace neutral rather than drug stimuli (Citation10,Citation21–Citation24). ABMT may decrease cue reactivity and/or increase ability to direct attention away from drug stimuli, both of which can be empirically measured using performance on various behavioral tasks and noninvasive neuroimaging techniques. Similarly, approach-avoidance training results in decreased brain activation in amygdala and mPFC to alcohol cues (Citation25). The current study used a random number generator to randomize 40 treatment-seeking participants with CUD to either ABMT or a control therapy group prior to pre-treatment assessment of attentional bias. In addition to assessment of attentional bias, participants completed cue reactivity (Citation12) and cognitive control (Citation15) fMRI tasks at baseline and post-intervention. Our first aim examined whether ABMT reduced drug cue reactivity within the cue reactivity or cognitive control network. Prior to the start of the trial, we hypothesized a greater effect would be present in the cue reactivity rather than cognitive network due to the more direct relationship that exists between the drug stimuli across cue reactivity and therapy tasks.

Methods

All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, and the applicable revisions at the time of the investigation. This study was approved by the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center Human Research Review Committee as HRRC#: 11-575 – “Attentional Bias Modification: Efficacy and Mechanisms of Action in Cocaine Addiction”.

Participants

The results from the clinical portion of this study have been previously reported (Citation10). Inclusion criteria included: 1) between 18–55 years old, 2) self-reported cocaine use in at least 4 of the prior 30 days (minimum of 3 continuous months of use), 3) seeking treatment and 4) not already in active treatment. Participants with self-reported history of major neurological disorder, medical condition, bipolar or schizophrenia spectrum disorder, contraindications for MRI, pregnancy, or active legal problems with potential for incarceration were excluded. This study was approved by an accredited IRB and written consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants were randomly assigned to ABMT or control therapy. A single therapeutic session of motivational interviewing was administered to all participants in both groups to reduce ethical concerns of non-treatment in the control group. Neuroimaging data (see for all days) were collected at 11.4 ± 6.1 days post-consent/motivational interviewing session (i.e., baseline) as well as approximately 1 week after the final ABMT or control therapy session (31.4 ± 10.1 days after initial assessment). Specifically, baseline neuroimaging data were obtained from 37 (ABMT = 19; control therapy = 18) participants (23 males; 38.3 ± 10.9 years of age). Two of these participants (1 from each task) were excluded for having greater than three times the inter-quartile range on framewise displacement (see Supplemental Table 1) for more than one translational or rotational parameter (Citation26). One additional participant was eliminated for poor performance (below 70.0% accuracy on the Stroop task), leaving a total of 36 participants in baseline craving task analysis and 35 in baseline Stroop task analysis. Post-intervention analyses included 32 participants (ABMT = 17; control therapy = 15) in both tasks due to study attrition.

Additional follow-up visits for collection of clinical data only occurred at approximately two weeks (short-term; 44.2 ± 11.0 days) and at three months (long-term; 120.2 ± 16.1 days), with results from these time-points previously presented (Citation10).

Clinical assessment

The Timeline Followback calendar (TLFB; Citation27) was used to determine self-reported cocaine, alcohol, and marijuana usage for all participants, as well as for any other self-reported drugs of use. Self-reported percent days of cocaine use was calculated for 30 days prior to baseline imaging, as well as 14 days prior to post-intervention, short-term follow-up and long-term follow-up. Each assessment was normalized for total number of days in each interval. See Supplemental Material and previous publication for all clinical materials.

ABMT task

The current task was largely adapted from ABMT studies in AUD (Citation24) both in terms of number of sessions and number of trials per session, as previously reported (see 10 and Supplemental Materials). Participants were pseudo-randomly presented with cocaine/neutral stimulus or neutral/neutral stimulus pairs (Supplemental Figure 1) to measure attentional bias. For the treatment sessions, probes replaced neutral stimuli on 100% of the trials in ABMT and 50% of the time in control therapy. The number of trials/sessions was equivalent in both groups, with each training session lasting approximately 22.5 minutes and each participant completing 5 separate sessions. The average time between sessions was approximately 5 days, with most individuals completing all 5 sessions in 3–4 weeks.

fMRI scanner tasks

All participants completed cue reactivity and cognitive control tasks at baseline and post-intervention while undergoing fMRI on a Siemens 3T scanner with a 12 channel head coil. Participants were asked to refrain from drinking alcohol or using illegal substances for 24 hours before the scan, as well as to refrain from smoking for an hour beforehand. Subjects practiced both tasks prior to entering the scanner environment. The craving task was identical to our previous publication (; Citation12), and involved 28 videos (duration: 12 ± 2 s) depicting cocaine and food consumption (e.g., someone snorting powder) or preparation (e.g., someone preparing a syringe). Participants viewed videos and continuously rated their desire to use cocaine on an 8-point Likert scale (0–7), with rating levels updated in real time. Subsequent craving ratings were used as a regressor for all voxel-wise analyses at the single subject level. The primary variables of interest from the cue reactivity task were behavioral (average maximum rating during each video) and neural responses for subjective craving, as well as a contrast comparing neural activation during cocaine versus food videos.

Figure 1. Diagrammatic representation of the neuroimaging tasks and behavioral results. During the cue reactivity task, participants observed videos depicting either the preparation or use of cocaine (CV) or food (FV) videos while continuously rating their cocaine craving on an 8 point Likert scale (0-7; Panel A). Box-and-whisker plots (Panel B) depict the average maximum drug craving during videos and in baseline periods following cocaine (CB) and food (FB) videos. The inter-block interval (IBI) and inter-trial interval (ITI) are also described. Box-and-whisker plots (Panel D) depict reaction times (RT) and accuracy for both attend-auditory (AA) and attend-visual (AV) conditions for auditory incongruent (AIT), auditory congruent (ACT), visual incongruent (VIT), and visual congruent (VCT) trials.

The cognitive control task involved a multisensory Stroop that was similar to our previous publication (; Citation15). Each block began with a cue word (“LOOK” = attend-visual; “HEAR” = attend-auditory) followed by a stream of simultaneously presented congruent or incongruent multisensory (auditory and visual) Arabic numerals (“ONE”, “TWO”, or “THREE”). Participants pressed a button corresponding to the target number in one modality while ignoring simultaneously presented distractors. Data were collapsed across attend-auditory and attend-visual trials. The primary variables of interest were behavioral (number of errors on incongruent trials, median reaction time [RT] difference between incongruent and congruent trials) and neural measures of cognitive control (percent signal change from incongruent minus congruent trials).

Analyses

For all behavioral and clinical data, normality was first assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Cohen’s d is provided for normally distributed data, with non-parametric effect sizes reported otherwise (Citation28).

MR imaging & statistical analysis

High resolution (1 × 1 × 1 mm) T1-weighted and echo-planar imaging [TR = 2000 ms; TE = 29 ms; flip angle = 75○; voxel size = 3.75 × 3.75 × 4.55] were collected during baseline and post-treatment sessions. Functional maps were calculated with the Analysis of Functional NeuroImages (AFNI) software package using standard pre-processing steps, including calculation of framewise displacement (Citation26). Percent signal change was calculated by averaging beta coefficients for images occurring during the peak hemodynamic response function and dividing by the average model intercept. Data from both tasks were spatially blurred using a 6 mm Gaussian full-width half-maximum filter and subsequently transformed to Talairach space.

Voxel-wise independent sample t-tests examined differences between groups at baseline for the cognitive control task (incongruent – congruent trials). Independent sample t-tests were also used to look at voxel-wise differences in drug cue reactivity (cocaine videos – food videos) or for associations with cocaine craving (ratings regressor). 2 × 2 mixed measures ANOVAs [Group (ABMT vs. control therapy) × Time (Baseline vs. Post-treatment)] examined the impact of treatment on functional activation while controlling for any potential baseline differences due to a failure in randomization. All functional results were corrected for false positives at p < .05 based on 10,000 Monte-Carlo simulations using spherical autocorrelation (p < .005; minimum cluster size = 2048 mm3). Cohen’s d maps were calculated between ABMT and control therapy groups for each task both at baseline and post-intervention (i.e., baseline – post-treatment; Supplemental Figures 2–5).

Associations with cocaine use

The second aim investigated whether baseline activity within the cue reactivity or cognitive control networks was associated with baseline or post-treatment cocaine use. Clinical predictors of change (baseline cocaine urine screen, baseline craving and baseline cocaine withdrawal severity) were entered first into the model (Citation29,Citation30). Regions of activation from both the cue reactivity and cognitive control tasks (see baseline imaging results) were entered in a second step. Cocaine use at baseline served as the dependent variable in the first model, whereas the difference between baseline and post-intervention cocaine use served as the dependent variable in the second model.

Results

Baseline clinical and imaging tasks

Published results (Citation10) indicated no significant differences between ABMT and control therapy participants (p > .10; See Supplemental Table 2) on major demographic, psychiatric or drug use variables at baseline (Cohen’s d range: −0.60–0.23) or for attentional bias measures (Cohen’s d range 0.11–0.23) suggesting that randomization was effective. Results from the cue reactivity task (cocaine versus food cues) indicated a higher maximum craving rating () for cocaine relative to food videos (t35 = 2.72, p = .01) as well as for the baseline period following videos (t35 = 4.18, p < .001) across both groups of participants, with no observable group differences for either cue type (all p’s > 0.10; Cohen’s d videos = 0.13–0.23; Cohen’s d baselines = −0.11–0.19). There were no group-wise differences in terms of baseline functional maps for either the cocaine minus food videos or for the craving regressor. There were no sizeable regions exhibiting large effect sizes for the group effect (see Supplemental Figure 2).

Across both control therapy and ABMT groups, cocaine videos resulted in greater activation relative to food videos () within the right orbitofrontal cortex/inferior frontal gyrus (OFC/IFG; BAs 11/13/45/46/47; 4028 mm3); right (BAs 6/9; 3357 mm3) and left (BA 6; 2735 mm3) premotor cortex/frontal eye fields (FEF); right middle temporal gyrus (MTG; BAs 20/21; 2148 mm3); right (BAs 7/13/19/22/37/39/40; 21505 mm3) and left (BAs 7/19/37/39/40; 37255 mm3) inferior (IPL) and posterior parietal cortex (PPC) extending into extrastriate visual cortex; bilateral ventral striatum (VS) and thalami (5176 mm3); bilateral thalamic and sub-thalamic nuclei (3760 mm3); and bilateral cerebellum (23954 mm3). Cocaine videos resulted in activation (operationally defined as positive BOLD signal above baseline) whereas food videos resulted in deactivation (negative BOLD signal below baseline) in bilateral anterior cingulate (ACC) extending into medial/superior frontal gyrus (BAs b. 6/8/9, l. 10/32; 16490 mm3), regions previously described as the anterior hub of the default-mode network (DMN). A similar pattern of greater deactivation for food relative to cocaine videos was also observed in posterior DMN, including the cingulate and posterior cingulate gyrus (BAs b. 7/23/24/31, l. 30; 11813 mm3). Finally, greater deactivation was observed for cocaine relative to food videos in the right insula (BAs 6/13/43; 2091 mm3). These results are summarized in Supplemental Table 3. The craving regressor was not associated with significant change from baseline activation anywhere in the brain.

Effects of intervention on functional and behavioral data

Previous results (Citation10) indicated no differences between ABMT and control therapy groups on any of the behavioral measures of attentional bias (treatment-related Cohen’s d range: −0.20–0.02). Similarly, both groups demonstrated statistically similar reductions in the normalized days of cocaine use post-intervention (ABMT = −17.3 ± 23.7%; control therapy = −6.0 ± 26.6%). A series of 2 × 2 [Group (ABMT vs. control therapy) × Time (Pre- vs. Post-intervention)] mixed ANOVAs were conducted to determine whether ABMT independently affected behavioral data or neural activity during the cue reactivity and Stroop tasks. Behavioral and functional results (Supplemental Figure 4) from the cue reactivity task were negative for both the main effect of Group (treatment-related Cohen’s d for maximum craving = 0.23) and the Group × Time interaction. There was a significant effect of Time for behavioral data, with a reduction in maximum craving during cocaine videos between baseline and post-intervention visits (F1,30 = 5.02, p = .033). The main effect of Time was not significant for functional results.

The main effect of Group and Group × Time interaction were also negative for the Stroop functional data (Supplemental Figure 5). A significant effect of Time within the rostral ACC (BAs 10/32; 3374 mm3) resulted from a reduced difference between incongruent and congruent trial activation post-intervention (). However, a zero-order correlation between changes in rostral ACC activation and cocaine use between baseline and post-intervention imaging sessions showed that the two were unrelated (r = −0.02; p > .10). There were no significant behavioral differences in RT (p > .10; treatment-related Cohen’s d range = −0.27 – −0.18) or accuracy (nonparametric effect size range = 0.37–0.45).

Association between baseline functional activity and cocaine use

Hierarchical regression models examined whether neural activation during either the cue reactivity or cognitive control networks was associated with cocaine use at baseline or post-intervention. All regions exhibiting increased activation for cocaine (C) relative to food (F) cues (OFC/IFG, FEF, MTG, IPL/PPC; VS, thalami/subthalamic nuclei, and cerebellum: r range = 0.27–0.83) were collapsed into a single variable using a weighted mean based on cluster volume. The right insula (C < F < 0) and posterior (F < C < 0) and anterior (F < 0 < C) DMN were included as separate predictor variables given their different patterns of activation in response to cocaine versus food cues. All significant regions for the Stroop task were collapsed due to similar activation (incongruent > congruent trials) patterns (VLPFC, DLPFC, ACC, insula, IPL/PPC, thalami/subthalamic nuclei and cerebellum) and high correlations among these regions (r range = 0.70–091).

Results (exact statistics presented in ) indicated that clinical variables accounted for significant variance in terms of normalized baseline cocaine use, with the baseline urine screen significantly and positively associated with increased baseline cocaine use. Although functional activation within the cue reactivity and cognitive control networks did not account for significant variance above and beyond clinical variables (R2 change = 0.16; p > .10), the degree of deactivation (cocaine cues – food cues) within the right insula was significantly associated with baseline use, whereas cue reactivity network activity was associated with baseline use only at a trend level.

Results () from the second hierarchical regression indicated that baseline clinical variables were not significantly associated with post-intervention use (i.e., baseline – post-intervention; larger magnitude = greater reduction in use). However, baseline activation was significantly associated with the decrease in cocaine use immediately post-treatment above and beyond clinical variables (R2 change = 0.46; p = .003). Specifically, the degree of activation (cocaine – food cues) in the aDMN and deactivation within the right insula were both significant, with a greater reduction in use associated with smaller differences in activation in the insula and aDMN between cocaine and food cues.

Discussion

Prominent theories of drug addiction suggest both enhanced neurophysiological response to drug-related cues in conjunction with weakened neurophysiological strength during cognitive control, both of which likely contribute to drug-seeking behaviors (Citation6). However, few fMRI studies have explicitly examined the efficacy of behavioral treatments in “normalizing” anomalous activation on independent measures of cue reactivity and cognitive control in participants with CUD as has been done with other substances (Citation31,Citation32). Similar to our null behavioral findings (Citation10), there was no support that ABMT affected functional activation during cue reactivity and multisensory cognitive control tasks (Aim 1). Functional activation from different nodes of the cue reactivity and DMN predicted both baseline and post-intervention cocaine use (Aim 2).

Previously published results (Citation10) indicated no differences between ABMT and control therapy for affecting cocaine use (i.e., both equally reduced use) or other drug-use behaviors (craving symptoms, withdrawal, etc.). Results from the current experiment suggest that ABMT did not differentially affect independent measures of behavior or neural activity (i.e., no Group × Time interaction) during cue reactivity or cognitive control tasks. This is not unexpected given our negative findings for ABMT altering neither attentional bias nor drug use behaviors (Citation10). Although functional activation within the rostral ACC was reduced for incongruent relative to congruent trials, this occurred equally across both ABMT and control therapies and was not associated with a reduction in cocaine use post-treatment. Recent meta-analyses highlight the existence of both positive and negative findings following ABMT (Citation33–Citation35), with other findings suggesting the potential moderating/mediating effects of symptom severity (Citation36,Citation37), disease characteristics (Citation38), presence of external stressors (Citation38) and difficulty in disambiguating cognitive constructs (Citation37) in determining ABMT efficacy.

Thus, previous observations of reduced cocaine use (Citation10) likely result from repeated exposure to cocaine stimuli or participants’ inherent desire to reduce use (Citation39–Citation42) rather than as a result of neural changes during Stroop or cue reactivity tasks. Recent studies highlight the role of symptom severity (Citation36,Citation37), disease characteristics (Citation38), presence of external stressors (Citation38) and difficulty in disambiguating cognitive constructs (Citation37) in moderating the efficacy of ABMT across various drug-abusing samples. All of these challenge the initial views of efficacy, with additional studies required to determine whether ABMT represents a viable mechanism for reducing cocaine use and/or associated behaviors in participants with CUD.

To our knowledge, the current study represents the first attempt to directly compare whether baseline activation within the cue reactivity or cognitive control network was associated with baseline or post-treatment use in the same sample of participants with CUD. Results suggest a stronger association between cocaine use and functional activation during the cue reactivity relative to cognitive control task. Specifically, participants exhibited the expected pattern of slower response times and increased activation within the ventrolateral, dorsolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, anterior insula, multisensory pSTS, and inferior parietal lobule for incongruent relative to congruent trials as has been observed in several previous studies (Citation15,Citation43,Citation44). Thus, although the task was efficacious in activating the requisite multisensory cognitive control network, the degree of functional activation was not associated with either cocaine use at baseline or the reduction in use following treatment. Differences in findings between the current and previous cognitive control studies (Citation18–Citation20) may be driven by sample-specific factors (e.g., in-patient versus out-patient), treatment seeking status, and prediction of reductions in use versus relapse (i.e., a binary variable).

Increased activation was observed in orbitofrontal gyrus, ventral striatum, cerebellum and inferior parietal lobule for cocaine relative to food videos across both ABMT and control therapy groups. These findings are not novel but are similar to previous results (Citation11,Citation12,Citation45,Citation46), and suggest that drug stimuli activate mesocortical and striatal dopaminergic pathways even when contrasted with an appetitive control. In addition, current findings provide an independent replication of our finding that participants with CUD fail to deactivate both the anterior and posterior nodes of the DMN during the presentation of cocaine but not food videos (Citation12). The magnitude of task-induced deactivations in the DMN parametrically increase (i.e., increased deactivation) with increasing attentional demands (Citation47) and one would expect that this would generalize to stimulus saliency. Paradoxically, participants with CUD exhibited activation within the DMN for cocaine cues relative to deactivation for food stimuli with lingering craving during baseline states. Therefore, it is possible that exposure to the cocaine videos causes participants with CUD to remain in a constant state of craving following exposure (Citation12), which in turn affects DMN deactivation.

The degree of deactivation in the right insula was associated with baseline cocaine use above and beyond traditional and more inexpensive clinical methods (Citation29,Citation30). Moreover, the degree of deactivation in the insula and activation within aDMN at baseline also predicted general treatment response across both the ABMT and control therapy groups even though traditional metrics obtained at baseline were not sensitive. These findings are similar to previous studies reporting that increased activation for cocaine cues predicted worse treatment outcome (Citation11,Citation16). In comparison to more inexpensive methods (e.g., drug screens and baseline consumption), fMRI has the potential for providing targeted areas for tailored interventions (i.e., pharmacological or neurostimulation) as well as identifying potential subgroups (i.e., relapse as a result of increased cue reactivity versus decreased inhibitory control) rather than just a general indication that the patient has relapsed.

Several limitations of the current experiment should be noted. Foremost, our sample size was equivalent to previously published studies (Citation16,Citation20,Citation24,Citation48) but still relatively modest, potentially limiting our ability to detect more subtle findings as well as to interrogate individual nodes from each network in regression analyses. Effect sizes are therefore presented for all clinical and imaging findings, and were typically in the small to moderate range. Second, the current study assessed cocaine use based on self-report rather than daily urine screens. Third, our study was performed in outpatients, 56.8% of whom had a positive urine screen at the time of the initial scan. Previous outcome prediction studies have involved either patients who had at least a week of cocaine abstinence (i.e., were in residential treatment; 11,18,19,20) or were confirmed abstinent by urine screen at the time of the scan (Citation16). Finally, we did not restrict food intake prior to scanning sessions such that the appetitive controls may not have been as powerful as if participants had been food restricted.

Conclusion

In summary, current results suggest that baseline activity within the right insula and aDMN was associated with baseline drug use and reduction above and beyond standard clinical metrics. These results highlight the sensitivity of functional activation for predicting real world use behaviors and provide an objective mechanism for target engagement. In contrast, activity within the cognitive control network was not associated with cocaine use at baseline or post-treatment, suggesting that deficits in cognitive control may not be as sensitive as cue reactivity for predicting cocaine use. Alternatively, different subtypes may exist among participants with participants with CUD (e.g., those with and without response inhibition deficits), and this could impact on how functional abnormalities are expressed. ABMT also did not differentially affect drug-use behaviors or neurocircuitry, suggesting that additional work is needed to establish treatment validity in participants with CUD.
