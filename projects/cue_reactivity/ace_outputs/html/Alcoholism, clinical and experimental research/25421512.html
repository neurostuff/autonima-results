Abstract

Background

Effect sizes of pharmacotherapy in alcoholism are modest. They might improve if subjects could be divided into more homogeneous subgroups and would then be treated targeted to their neurobiological profile. In such an effort, we tested neural cue reactivity as a potential predictor of treatment response to naltrexone. Alcohol-associated cues cause brain activations in mesocorticolimbic networks due to the positive reinforcing properties of alcohol. These activations were reported to be associated with relapse behavior. Naltrexone, an antagonist at the mu-opioid receptor, improves drinking behavior in some but not all patients probably by blocking the positive reinforcement of alcohol. Conversely, acamprosate is proposed to modulate negative reinforcement (withdrawal and cue-induced withdrawal). Identifying subjects with elevated cue reactivity and testing their response to medical treatment could thus improve our understanding of some of the mechanisms underlying pharmacotherapy response.

Methods

A picture-perception task featuring alcohol-related and neutral stimuli was presented to 64 recently detoxified alcohol-dependent patients. Patients came from 1 center of a larger double-blind randomized multicenter clinical trial (the “PREDICT Study”). They were scanned prior to being randomized to either naltrexone or acamprosate. We examined the interaction between medication and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) cue reactivity, as measured by the percentage of voxels activated, using the time to the first severe relapse as the outcome criterion. Our a priori formulated hypothesis was that naltrexone but not acamprosate should be efficacious in subjects with high cue reactivity.

Results

We observed an interaction effect between pretreatment brain activation induced by alcohol images and medication (acamprosate/naltrexone) on relapse behavior. In line with our hypothesis, this interaction was driven by treatment response to naltrexone in patients with elevated pretreatment cue reactivity in the ventral striatum.

Conclusions

fMRI has the potential for predicting treatment response to naltrexone in a subgroup of alcohol-dependent patients. However, this approach will be limited to researching the mechanisms and principles of treatment response.

Pharmacotherapy is a successful treatment in alcohol use disorders, but effect sizes in clinical trials are small (Anton et al., 2006, 2012; Heilig et al., 2011; Rosner et al., 2010). Results might improve if heterogeneous samples of subjects could be divided into more homogeneous subgroups, which would then receive treatment targeted to their neurobiological profile (Litten et al., 2012). Naltrexone in patients with the OPRM1 A118G genotype is an example of 1 such approach suggested by better outcomes in G-allele carriers than in homozygous A-allele carriers (Anton et al., 2008; Heilig et al., 2011; Oslin et al., 2003). In this paper, we report results of an additional way of predefining patients likely to respond to naltrexone. Naltrexone, a mu-opioid receptor antagonist, reduces the positive reinforcing effects of alcohol and/or the anticipation of such effects as measured by subjective craving (“blocking the buzz”) (Heilig et al., 2010, 2011). Naltrexone decreases functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) cue reactivity in the ventral striatum (VS) of nontreatment-seeking alcoholics (Myrick et al., 2008). Furthermore, the VS has been shown to be a core region in the brain's reward system and to be involved in anticipating the reinforcing effects of alcohol (Bühler and Mann, 2011; Heinz, 2002; Koob and Volkow, 2010; Schacht et al., 2011, 2013).

One rationale for our study was derived from the incentive-sensitization model of addiction (Robinson and Berridge, 1993). It suggests that addictive drugs alter mesolimbic brain networks that mediate the attribution of incentive salience. These neural circuits may then become sensitized to the effects of specific drugs as well as to drug-associated stimuli such as drug paraphernalia and advertisements or drug-related environments. In this way, drug-associated stimuli become salient and can cause pathological “wanting” to take the drug. A bias of attentional processing toward drug-associated stimuli develops. In previous studies, we found associations between attentional bias for alcohol cues and pretreatment alcohol consumption (Vollstädt-Klein et al., 2009), which was pronounced in earlier stages of dependence (Loeber et al., 2009). Attentional bias to alcohol cues persists during intoxication (Miller and Fillmore, 2011) and might be increased after a priming dose of alcohol (Schoenmakers et al., 2008). Drug-associated stimuli may also acquire the ability to evoke drug-like responses (Drummond, 2001; Monti et al., 2000). The “cue reactivity” construct comprises reactions to drug-associated stimuli on various levels, including subjective (e.g., drug craving), behavioral (e.g., drug seeking), and physiological responses (e.g., changes in heart rate and skin conductance) (Drummond, 2001). Earlier work including imaging studies suggest an association between cue reactivity and drug taking (Grüsser et al., 2004; Weiss et al., 2001) and intensity of patients' self-reported drug craving (Maas et al., 1998; Myrick et al., 2004; Smolka et al., 2006).

In a prospective design, we used fMRI to determine patients' brain activity in response to alcohol cues versus neutral cues to then test the effect of naltrexone and acamprosate on relapse behavior in patients with different degrees of brain activation. This approach was embedded into a prospective clinical trial (the “PREDICT Study”; Mann et al., 2009, 2013). We hypothesized that the group of patients who had higher levels of alcohol-associated pretreatment cue reactivity in reward-related areas such as the VS would respond better to naltrexone than the group with low or no cue reactivity. Conversely, acamprosate is not believed to influence the positive reinforcement effects of alcohol, but to modulate brain glutamate levels (Hermann et al., 2012; Lingford-Hughes and Nutt, 2003; Spanagel et al., 2005). For this reason, we did not expect high cue reactivity in the VS to predict positive outcomes in patients randomized to acamprosate (for detailed a priori hypotheses, see Mann et al., 2009). This hypothesis has been supported by a recent fMRI study (Langosch et al., 2012). Langosch and colleagues (2012) did not find an additional benefit of acamprosate treatment in decreasing cue reactivity compared with inpatient treatment alone. We further addressed a potential confounder when using fMRI cue reactivity as an endophenotype, which might be unrelated to the OPRM1 genotype-stratification to identify naltrexone responders. In other words, we wanted to examine whether the allelic frequency of the mutated 118G allele of the OPRM1 gene might be a confounder, or whether the influence of cue reactivity is additive.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The results reported here were collected within the framework of a larger, double-blind, randomized, multicenter trial (the PREDICT Study; Mann et al., 2009, 2013). Alcohol-dependent patients (DSM-IV; American Psychiatric Association, 1994) who had been abstinent and without withdrawal symptoms for at least 1 week were randomly assigned to naltrexone, acamprosate, or placebo. Patients for the fMRI substudy were only recruited in Mannheim due to reasons of technical infrastructure in the other centers (see flowchart in Fig. S1). The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine Mannheim of the University of Heidelberg. All participants provided written informed consent according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosis according to DSM-IV and ICD-10 (assessed with the Structured Clinical Interview [SCID-I], German version; Wittchen et al., 1997), abstinence between 4 and 28 days, consumption of more than 14 drinks (women) or more than 21 drinks (men) per week during a consecutive 30-day period prior to admission. Only right-handed subjects (assessed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory; Oldfield, 1971) with a normal or corrected to normal vision (binocular visual acuity ≥0.8) were included. Exclusion criteria comprised history of other substance abuse (apart from alcohol, nicotine, and cannabis), psychiatric disorders requiring medication and “unstable medical conditions.” Patients were assessed with a standardized battery of questionnaires, structured interviews, including current patterns of alcohol, tobacco and drug consumption, liver parameters, and sociodemographic variables (Mann et al., 2009).

At the end of an inpatient medical detoxification period lasting between 14 and 21 days, each patient was randomly assigned to the naltrexone, acamprosate, or placebo group in proportions 2:2:1. Patient allocation to the treatment arms was based on an imbalanced block randomization code generated by means of an SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) procedure provided by the biometrician in charge of the project. Pharmacotherapy was applied for 12 weeks in a daily dosage of 2 g for acamprosate and 50 mg for naltrexone. All patients received counseling over a total of 6 months and additional follow-up assessment for another year resulting in an observation period of 18 months after treatment initiation.

In the now presented subsample of patients, we measured fMRI cue reactivity after withdrawal symptoms had subsided but before the first dose of medication had been administered. A second scan was conducted following 2 weeks of medication treatment (data will be reported separately).

Data from 6 participants were excluded from statistical analysis because of heavy movement during the first scanner session. The final sample consisted of 36 patients in the naltrexone group (age 45 ± 10 years, range 27 to 63 years, 29 male, 27 smokers) and 28 patients in the acamprosate group (age 41 ± 6 years, range 32 to 60 years, 22 male, 25 smokers). Due to the imbalanced randomization of the whole study (see above and Mann et al., 2009), few placebo patients were expected for the fMRI substudy presented here. Therefore, controlling for placebo effects was not intended and patients randomized to placebo were not analyzed.

Assessment of Drinking-Related Parameters

Severity of alcohol dependence was measured by the Alcohol Dependence Scale (ADS; Skinner and Horner, 1984). Alcohol craving was assessed by the Alcohol Urge Questionnaire (AUQ; Bohn et al., 1995). Besides the assessment with the SCID-I interview (German version; Wittchen et al., 1997), alcohol use disorder was examined with the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Reinert and Allen, 2002). Furthermore, a questionnaire of obsessive-compulsive alcohol-related thoughts and behavior was completed by all participants (Obsessive-Compulsive Drinking Scale [OCDS]; Anton, 2000; Mann and Ackermann, 2000). Alcohol consumption was assessed with the Form90 interview (Miller and Del Boca, 1994; Scheurich et al., 2005). These drinking-related parameters as well as the number of previous detoxifications did not differ between the 2 groups (see Table 1). Abstinence rates during the 12 weeks' period of medical treatment did also not differ between naltrexone (50.0%) and acamprosate (53.6%) patients.

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was prepared from whole blood according to standard procedures. Genotyping of rs1799971 was performed by primer extension reaction chemistry, using matrix-assisted, laser desorption/ionization, time of flight mass-spectrometry performed on the iPLEX Assay (Sequenom, San Diego, CA) and with a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA; assay-on-demand ID C_8950074_1).

Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Paradigm

We applied a well-established fMRI task also used in previous studies (Hermann et al., 2006). During fMRI, we presented 15 alcohol-associated and 30 control stimuli (15 affectively neutral and 15 neutral abstract stimuli generated by scrambling the alcohol-associated cues). Pictures of all categories were comparable in color distribution, contrast, and complexity. Furthermore, alcohol-associated and neutral stimuli were matched for complexity. The alcohol stimuli from our own picture battery evoke craving and cue-induced mesocorticolimbic brain activation in alcohol-dependent patients (Grusser et al., 2000; Wrase et al., 2002). The affectively neutral stimuli were drawn from the International Affective Picture System (Lang et al., 2008). Visual cues were presented in a block design (3 pictures per category, 19.8 seconds per block, see Fig. 1), resulting in 5 blocks per category. The length of the intertrial interval, during which a fixation cross was presented, was 19.8 seconds. A total of 218 scans were acquired, resulting in task duration of 12 minutes.

Before and after the scanner session, craving for alcohol was measured using a visual analog scale.

fMRI Data Acquisition

Scanning was performed using a 1.5-T clinical whole-body tomography (Magnetom VISION; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a standard quadrature head coil. For functional data acquisition, 24 slices were acquired every 3.3 seconds (4 mm thickness, 1 mm gap) using a standard echoplanar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 1.8 ms, TE = 66 ms, α = 90°) with an in-plane resolution of 64 × 64 pixels with a field of view (FOV) of 220 × 220 mm2. To reduce susceptibility artifacts, T2*-weighted EPI were acquired in a transversal orientation tilted by 30° clockwise to AC-PC-line. A morphological 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) image data set (1 × 1 × 1 mm3 voxel size, FOV 256 × 256 mm2, 162 slices, TR = 11.4 ms, TE = 4.4 ms, α = 12°) was acquired for anatomical reference.

fMRI Data Analysis

The fMRI data were analyzed with SPM5 (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK). The first 5 volumes of each functional time series were discarded to eliminate T1 effects. All volumes were realigned to the remaining first volume to correct for between-scan movements. After motion correction, we excluded all subjects with more than 3 mm/3° head movement/rotation. The functional images were spatially normalized to a standard EPI template using a 12-parameter affine transformation with additional nonlinear components. The functional data were smoothed by using an isotropic Gaussian kernel (12 mm full width at half maximum). First-level statistical analyses were performed by modeling alcohol versus control conditions (boxcar functions convolved with the hemodynamic response function) as explanatory variables within the context of the general linear model on a voxel-by-voxel basis. To detect cue-induced brain activation in the whole group of patients (placebo, acamprosate, and naltrexone treatment), we included the individual contrast images (alcohol vs. control pictures) in a 1-sample t-test. The significance level was set to p < 0.05, Familywise error rate (FWE) corrected, with a minimum cluster threshold of 20 contiguous voxels. For our main region of interest (ROI), the VS, we applied ROI analyses using FWE-correction with p < 0.05 using an anatomically mask, downloaded from the Nielsen and Hansen database (Nielsen and Hansen, 2002) and converted to a binary mask with a threshold of ≥0.025. For further analysis examining the association between cue-induced activation in the VS, medication, and treatment outcomes, we calculated the percent of activated voxels (p < 0.05 uncorrected on the individual first level) in the VS for each participant. This aggregation method is an established procedure for fMRI in general, which is for example implemented in the SPM toolbox MarsBar (Brett et al., 2002) (http://marsbar.sourceforge.net/). We extracted the percent of activated voxels with a self-written SPM toolbox. The percentage of activated voxels reported in this article was predetermined as our measure of choice. However, out of a more methodological interest, we also did further analyses using several alternative methods to aggregate the whole-brain data for the VS mask. These were the unweighted mean of contrast estimates, the weighted mean of contrast estimates using probabilistic masks, and a more sophisticated measure summing up t-values above an individual threshold. Results for the different aggregation methods were similar but differed in explained variation. Details will be reported in a different paper that focuses on methodology.

Statistical Analysis

The clinical outcome measure was defined in terms of time to first heavy relapse (5 or more drinks per day for men, 4 or more drinks per day for women) during the period of medical treatment (i.e., 12 weeks). To test our main hypothesis of an interaction between cue-reactivity levels and pharmacological interventions, we used a Cox regression analysis in SPSS (Statistical Package of the Social Sciences, Version 20; IBM Corp., Somers, NY) for the time to first severe relapse with the main effects of VS cue reactivity as covariate and the factor medication together with the corresponding interaction. In a second analysis, we added the ADS score as a covariate of no interest to control for the severity of dependence. Because results did not change, these data are not reported. For illustration purposes, we further examined the efficacy of naltrexone and acamprosate in relation to cue reactivity in the VS by comparing the survival curves of participants with high versus low VS cue reactivity using a log rank test in SPSS (after median split using the percentages of activated VS voxels). An additional Cox regression analysis was conducted to examine the influence of craving increase during the scanner session on relapse risk as well its interaction with medication. The association between VS activation and craving increase was assessed using a linear correlation.

To confirm the specificity of the effects in the VS, we estimated the percentage of activated voxels in the middle occipital gyrus as a control region, which demonstrated a task main effect (see Table 2), but in which we did not expect an interaction between treatment and cue-induced activation. With these data, we applied a similar Cox regression analysis like for the VS. To control for potential confounders based on the allelic frequency of the mutated 118G allele of the OPRM1 gene between patients with high and low cue reactivity, we conducted chi-square tests.

Results

Alcohol-dependent patients showed higher activation in response to alcohol-related images than they did to control cues in many brain regions, such as parts of the visual system; the insula; the thalamus; the supplementary motor area; the inferior, middle, and medial prefrontal cortices; the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC); the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and mesocorticolimbic areas such as the VS (see Table 2 and Fig. 2).

In the VS, which had been predefined as our core ROI, we found an interaction effect between medication and pretreatment cue-induced fMRI brain activation on time to first severe relapse (Cox regression, χ2 = 5.155, df = 1, p = 0.023). Here, the results for the main effects were p = 0.009 for VS cue reactivity (χ2 = 6.910, df = 1) and p = 0.275 for medication (χ2 = 1.191, df = 1), whereas no significant interaction effect was found in the control region (visual cortex, p = 0.613). The interaction is expressed in terms of declining hazard ratios: with increasing VS cue reactivity, the risk of relapse decreased in patients assigned to naltrexone compared to those treated with acamprosate. To further illustrate this interaction, we performed a median split using the percentage of activated VS voxels followed by comparing patients with high versus low VS cue reactivity, separate for each medication group. Naltrexone-treated patients with high VS cue reactivity prior to treatment showed significantly better outcomes than patients on naltrexone showing less VS cue reactivity (after median split, p = 0.014, log rank test) supporting our hypothesis. As expected, we found no association between VS cue reactivity and time to relapse for patients assigned to acamprosate (after median split, p = 0.115, log rank test). Figure 3 displays the relapse rates for naltrexone and acamprosate patients, comparing participants with low to those with high cue reactivity in the VS. Results did not change when severity of dependence (for assessment details, see Supporting Information) was added as a covariate. While there was a positive association between craving increase during the scanner session and cue-induced VS activation (r = 0.337 p = 0.004), we did neither find an interaction between this increase in craving and medication on relapse nor a main effect of craving.

The allelic frequency of the mutated 118G allele of the OPRM1 gene did not differ between patients with high and low cue reactivity in the entire sample (p = 0.451) or in the groups of patients assigned to naltrexone (p = 0.678) or acamprosate (p = 0.683) (see Table S1).

Discussion

The efficacy of naltrexone- and acamprosate-based pharmacotherapies could increase if it were possible to target medications to subgroups of patients displaying a neurobiologically defined profile that was associated with response to a specific medication (Litten et al., 2012). As both medications in question act by different mechanisms, the potential for a differential treatment approach was suggested (details in Heilig et al., 2011; Mann et al., 2013). Naltrexone is suggested to block the positive reinforcement of alcohol, whereas acamprosate is proposed to modulate negative reinforcement. In other words, effect sizes could improve if we could stratify patients to more homogeneous subgroups instead of using the same approach to treat the full sample of heterogeneous patients (“one size does not fit all”). So far, the most prominent although not undisputed way of stratifying patients has been to use the OPRM1 A118G genotype (Kranzler and Edenberg, 2010; Oslin et al., 2003; reviewed in Heilig et al., 2011). In our own “PREDICT Study,” we put forward several a priori hypotheses (Mann et al., 2009) concerning the interaction of medications with opioidergic and glutamatergic genotypes. We now report an additional way of predefining patients likely to respond to naltrexone by using fMRI to measure patients' brain activity in response to alcohol cues versus neutral cues. In nontreatment-seeking alcoholics, naltrexone has already been shown to decrease fMRI cue reactivity in the VS (Myrick et al., 2008). Now, we are able to demonstrate the potential of this finding by showing that high cue reactivity may be an “endophenotype” (Jupp and Dalley, 2014) that can help to prospectively identify alcohol-dependent patients likely to respond to treatment with naltrexone. High cue reactivity was already suggested to be such an endophenotype for alcohol use disorder risk in an electroencephalography study (Bartholow et al., 2010). Good test–retest reliability of striatal cue-induced fMRI response further supports the existence of such an endophenotype (Schacht et al., 2011).

Advances in neuroimaging research have substantially enhanced our understanding of the neural underpinnings of alcoholism by identifying alcohol reward brain systems (Sullivan et al., 2010). Among the brain regions commonly implicated in cue-reactivity studies are the nucleus accumbens in the VS, the dorsal striatum, the amygdala, the thalamus, the hippocampus, the ACC, the visual cortices, the fusiform gyrus, the prefrontal cortex (especially the OFC), the motor cortex, and the insula (for a recent review, see Yalachkov et al., 2012). The brain network that was activated in our study corresponded to these regions, indicating that the cue-reactivity paradigm worked well.

In this study, the VS being a key region in the reward system was predefined as the main ROI. As mentioned previously, naltrexone had already been shown to reduce cue reactivity in this region in nontreatment-seeking alcoholics (Myrick et al., 2008). Additionally, in an earlier study, we showed an association between cue reactivity in the striatum and drinking at follow-up (Grüsser et al., 2004). Based on these findings, and the suggestion that naltrexone reduces the positive reinforcing effects of alcohol (Heilig et al., 2010, 2011), as well as the fact that the VS has a central role in anticipating the reinforcing effects of alcohol (Bühler and Mann, 2011; Heinz, 2002; Koob and Volkow, 2010; Schacht et al., 2011), we expected VS cue reactivity to be associated with naltrexone response.

Indeed, we found an interaction between treatment and cue-induced VS activation, on time to first severe relapse. This interaction was not found in a control region in the visual cortex, indicating an effect specific for the reward circuitry, in this case the VS. The interaction effect for the VS was driven by decreased relapse risk with increasing VS cue reactivity under naltrexone compared with acamprosate treatment. This result indicates that patients with high VS cue reactivity are more likely to respond to naltrexone treatment. Conversely, but in line with our hypothesis, high VS cue reactivity did not predict good treatment outcomes in acamprosate patients. As a glutamate-level modulator (Hermann et al., 2012; Lingford-Hughes and Nutt, 2003), acamprosate may affect alcohol-associated negative mood states instead.

As it has been shown that carriers of the 118G allele of the OPRM1 gene are more sensitive to naltrexone's effects compared with noncarriers (Oslin et al., 2003) and that 118G allele carriers demonstrate increased dopamine response to alcohol and stronger cue reactivity (Ramchandani et al., 2011; Wiers et al., 2009; van den Wildenberg et al., 2007), we checked whether our finding might be a result of a differential distribution of 118G allele carriers. However, the allelic frequency of the mutated 118G allele of the OPRM1 gene did not differ between patients exhibiting high and low cue reactivity, indicating that fMRI represents an unrelated endophenotype. Unfortunately, due to the limited sample size, it was not possible to examine whether there is an interaction between cue reactivity and allelic frequency, or if there is indeed an additive influence of cue reactivity. Although there was a positive association between craving increase during the scanner session and cue-induced VS activation, we did not find an influence of craving increase on relapse risk and no interaction between craving increase and medication. The lack of concordance could be a result of fMRI being a physiological measure, whereas self-reported craving is a subjective measure and thus more vulnerable, that is, in terms of social desirability.

It is still a matter of debate whether naltrexone works primarily by blocking ethanol's pharmacological-reward effects and/or whether it works by blocking cue-induced reinforcement (Rosner et al., 2010). While this study was not able to address the first proposition, our data suggest that naltrexone may indeed have the ability to interact with cue-induced response, lending supporting evidence to the second.

A limitation of our study is the lack of a placebo control. While having the fMRI substudy embedded in a large clinical trial may be seen as strength, the downside was that the restriction of the fMRI study to only 1 of the trial centers and to the 2:2:1 randomization code would not provide enough placebo patients. Because of the foreseeable lack of sufficient statistical power, we decided to refrain from analyzing placebo patients. Although the results reported earlier support our a priori hypothesis, they clearly need to be replicated in a placebo-controlled trial before final conclusions can be drawn.

A second more general limitation may be seen in the fact that inferences were made on the group level due to the used method, because fMRI is only suitable to draw conclusions on the group level. Therefore, caution should be used in generalizing them to specific cases.

In conclusion, there seems to be a role for functional imaging in further elucidating the mechanisms of action of treatment response in alcohol-dependent patients. Extending earlier work (Myrick et al., 2008) our fMRI study embedded into a randomized controlled trial indicates that fMRI cue reactivity may provide a way to better understand responses to naltrexone treatment. However, for obvious reasons, this approach will be largely limited to studying the mechanisms of treatment response. As a valuable tool for drug development, it can help to elucidate the neurobiology of relapse and its prevention by means of pharmacotherapy.
