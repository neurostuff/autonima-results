Abstract

It has been shown that in alcoholic patients, alcohol-related cues produce increased activation of reward-related brain regions like the ventral striatum (VS), which has been proposed as neurobiological basis of craving. Modulating this activation might be a promising option in the treatment of alcohol addiction. One approach might be real-time functional magnetic resonance imaging neurofeedback (rtfMRI NF). This study was set up to implement and evaluate a rtfMRI approach in a group of non-addicted heavy social drinkers. Thirty-eight heavy drinking students were assigned to a real feedback group (rFB, n = 13), a yoke feedback group (yFB, n = 13) and a passive control group (noFB, n = 12). After conducting a reward task as functional localizer to identify ventral striatal regions, the participants viewed alcohol cues during three NF training blocks in a 3 T MRI scanner. The rFB group received feedback from their own and the yFB from another participants' VS. The noFB group received no feedback. The rFB and the yFB groups were instructed to downregulate the displayed activation. Activation of the VS and prefrontal control regions was compared between the groups. We found significant downregulation of striatal regions specifically in the rFB group. While the rFB and the yFB groups showed significant activation of prefrontal regions during feedback, this activation was only correlated to the reduction of striatal activation in the rFB group. We conclude that rtfMRI NF is a suitable method to reduce striatal activation to alcohol cues. It might be a promising supplement to the treatment of alcoholic patients.

Introduction

Alcohol dependence is one of most frequent psychiatric diseases in Western industrial nations. Only about 10 percent of the patients receive a therapy. Even for those who get a therapy, the outcome is not satisfying because about 80 percent are affected by one or more relapses (Polich, Armor & Braiker 1980). The reasons for relapse are multifarious. Often discussed reasons are conditioned responses and related drug craving behavior. The incentive-sensitization model of addiction (Robinson & Berridge 1993) describes that previously neutral stimuli like contexts of habitual drinking behavior or the sight and smell of alcohol beverages become strongly associated with the reward value of alcohol during the development of the disease. Through this associative learning process, these former neutral stimuli, as now conditioned stimuli, are able to produce alcohol seeking, craving and relapse. This phenomenon is known as ‘cue-reactivity’ and has been observed in several studies focusing on behavioral, subjective and physiological levels (Carter & Tiffany 1999; Drummond 2000). Studies with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) propose an association between neural cue-reactivity in mesolimbic pathways and relapse (Grusser et al. 2004; Schacht, Anton & Myrick 2013). In addition to these mechanisms, it was proposed that attentional biases and the over-evaluation of the reward value of the drug reduce the value of natural, non-drug reinforcers. Moreover, addiction has been associated with a dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex. Goldstein & Volkow (2002, 2011) integrated this to the impaired response inhibition and salience attribution syndrome. This syndrome is defined as the combination of an altered reward system leading to the over-evaluation of drug rewards and a reduction of the value of non-drug reinforcers and deficits in response inhibition further facilitating the increased reactivity to drug-associated stimuli.

Based on these ideas, Volkow, Fowler & Wang (2004) proposed four strategies for interventions in addicted individuals: decreasing the reward value of the drug; increasing the salience of non-drug reinforcers; diminishing the conditioned drug responses; and strengthening inhibitory and executive control.

In previous studies, it has been shown that cue-exposure-based extinction training (CET), a psychotherapeutic intervention where patients are confronted with alcohol cues but may not show consummation behavior, exerts a positive effect reducing cue-reactivity (Drummond, Cooper & Glautier 1990). On the neural level, we demonstrated that CET can be used to reduce ventral striatal activation to alcohol-related cues (Vollstadt-Klein et al. 2011). However, it would be relevant to test whether a more active involvement of patients could produce comparable effects or, in combination with CET, increases its effects. One possible intervention, which has, to our knowledge, not been tested in this context so far, is real-time fMRI (rtfMRI) neurofeedback. Therefore, we conducted this first study to test whether it is possible to gain voluntary control over ventral striatal cue-reactivity by means of rtfMRI neurofeedback.

With the advancement of MRI technology, rtfMRI neurofeedback has been increasingly considered as a method to gain control over brain activation. Following up a long tradition in biofeedback methodology (Schwartz & Beatty 1977), rtfMRI neurofeedback uses the principles of operant conditioning to train individuals to gain control over particular brain regions or networks (Sulzer et al. 2013; Ruiz et al. 2014).

So far, the majority of studies in this area were restricted to healthy control subjects and focused on the modulation of brain regions associated with emotion regulation (Caria et al. 2010; Johnston et al. 2011; Zotev et al. 2011; Sarkheil et al. 2014), perception (Scharnowski et al. 2012) or movement (Bray, Shimojo & O'Doherty 2007; Yoo et al. 2008). The overall result of these studies was that healthy subjects learned to gain control over brain activation pattern, typically within a few training sessions.

However, there are also studies demonstrating the applicability of the method in clinical conditions like Parkinson's disease (Subramanian et al. 2011), chronic pain (deCharms et al. 2005), chronic tinnitus (Haller, Birbaumer & Veit 2010), depression (Linden et al. 2012), schizophrenia (Ruiz et al. 2013), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (Levesque, Beauregard & Mensour 2006) or adiposity (Frank et al. 2012). While these studies also found that patients can learn to gain control over their brain activation, there is no evidence for a sustained effect of the treatment so far.

In the field of substance dependence, first fMRI neurofeedback pilot studies were conducted in patients with nicotine addiction (Hartwell et al. 2013; Li et al. 2013). The authors developed two different approaches to reduce craving by means of rtfMRI. Inspired by findings from Brody et al. (2004) who showed that the craving reducing effect of Bupropion during the exposure of nicotine-associated stimuli was accompanied by a decreased activation of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), in the first approach, it was tried to control the ACC in order to reduce activation. The idea of the second approach was to strengthen the cortical control system (Hartwell et al. 2011). For this purpose, the activation of the middle prefrontal cortex (mPFC) as a part of the cortical control system should be increased. While for the first paradigm, the authors found reduced ACC activation, which was correlated with reduced craving, the participants failed to learn the modulation of the mPFC bold signal. In addition, the attempt to combine both approaches (Hartwell et al. 2013) and to reduce ACC activation and increase mPFC activation at the same time turned out to be too complex for the participants to learn.

In line with these first attempts to use rtfMRI neurofeedback to modulate cue-reactivity in the context of addiction, we designed a pilot study to explore the applicability of the method in the context of ventral striatal responses to alcohol cues. We used a well-established monetary reward paradigm (Kirsch et al. 2003, 2006) as functional localizer to identify the ventral striatal region of the brain reward system and afterwards tried to reduce the activation of this region by means of rtfMRI neurofeedback in a group of heavy social drinkers.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Thirty-eight heavy drinking students (29 male, mean age 24.11 years) participated after giving informed consent. Participants were selected from a large data set of students who completed an on-line version of alcohol use disorder identification test (AUDIT) (Saunders et al. 1993), a 10-item screening questionnaire that identifies individuals with harmful alcohol consumption behavior. Men with a score above 8 and women with a score above 5 are considered as showing risky drinking behavior. Students were invited to participate if their AUDIT score exceeded 8, independent of their gender.

All participants had normal or corrected to normal vision and no history of a major somatic disease or any neurological or psychiatric disorder. They were assigned to one of the three experimental groups. There was no significant difference in any of the demographic variables (Table 1).

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical Faculty Mannheim, University of Heidelberg.

Study design

Participants underwent a one session rtfMRI neurofeedback training which lasted for 1.5 hours. First they completed a monetary reward paradigm (Kirsch et al. 2003, 2006), which served as functional localizer. Afterward, a three-dimensional (3D) anatomical scan of the brain was conducted. Finally, three neurofeedback training blocks were presented. The three groups differed in terms of the feedback received. One group (experimental real feedback group, rFB) received feedback from a region of interest (ROI) in their ventral striatum (VS). The ROI was defined individually using the data of the functional localizer session, the region with the strongest response was chosen, independent of the side of activation within the VS. One control group received a yoke feedback (yFB) from another participant of the rFB group and the second control group, which was added to control for simple habituation effects in the VS, was instructed just to view the pictures without feedback (noFB).

Tasks

Reward paradigm

A shortened version of our repeatedly used monetary reward task (Kirsch et al. 2003, 2006) was presented as a functional localizer to identify the VS in each participant individually. In this paradigm, three cues are presented to the participants. A ‘win’ cue, announcing a reaction time (RT) task where fast responses are rewarded by money, a ‘verbal’ cue, announcing a RT task where only verbal feedback is given, and a control cue, which is not followed by a task. The activation of the bilateral VS elicited by this paradigm has been found to be highly reliable (Plichta et al. 2012). For details of the paradigm, see Supporting Information.

Neurofeedback

Participants conducted three neurofeedback training sessions and one transfer session without feedback (rFB and yFB groups only). During the training session, alcohol-related pictures were presented on the left side of the display. On the right side, the feedback was presented using a thermometer display (Fig. 1), reflecting VS activation by means of 10 vertical bars. The value displayed at a time was calculated as the percentage change of the VS activation compared with the mean activation during the preceding baseline phase. Each vertical bar of the thermometer represented a 0.2 percent signal change. The participants were instructed to downregulate the thermometer by means of any strategy they like. In the rFB group, the activation of the participant's VS was presented, whereas in the yFB group, the activation displayed came from another participant of the rFB group. In the noFB group, the participants were instructed just to passively view the display. Here, the thermometer display showed a constant mean value. Each training session consisted of six alcohol-related pictures each followed by a rest period displaying a fixation cross. Each picture and the fixation cross were presented for 40 seconds, resulting in a duration of 9 minutes per training session. The participants were asked about their favored alcoholic drink (beer or wine or beer and wine) and stimuli were presented from this class of beverages. For the transfer session, the instruction was to use the previously successful strategies but no feedback was given.

MRI data acquisition

Scanning was performed using a 3 T whole body tomograph (MAGNETON Tim Trio; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). For the reward paradigm, 202 T2*-weighted echo-planar images [repetition time (TR) = 2 seconds, echo time (TE) = 30 milliseconds, flip angle = 80°, 28 transversal slices, slice thickness = 4 mm, 0.5 mm gap, voxel dimensions = 3 × 3 × 4 m3, field of view (FOV) = 192 × 192 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64] were acquired in descending order. For the neurofeedback, 268 volumes were collected using the same parameters as for the reward paradigm. Images were presented via MR compatible video goggles (Resonance Technology Inc., Los Angeles, CA, USA). Task presentation was performed using the Presentation® software package (Version 9.9, Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Albany, CA, USA). Real-time fMRI analysis including on-line movement correction was conducted using Turbo-BrainVoyager 3.0 software (Brain Innovations, Maastricht, The Netherlands). The ROI for the feedback signal was manually drawn after visual inspection of the reward paradigm activation as revealed by Turbo-BrainVoyager. The activation of this ROI was then shown to the participant as a thermometer display during the training blocks.

In addition, a T1-weighted anatomical measurement of the whole brain was performed (TR = 2.3 seconds, TE = 3.03 milliseconds, flip angle = 13°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, 192 slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel dimensions = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, matrix size = 256 × 256).

MRI data analysis

fMRI data were additionally analyzed off-line to allow for a more precise preprocessing and to conduct group analysis. All fMRI off-line analyses were conducted with the SPM8 software package (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK) implemented in Matlab (MathWorks Inc., Sherborn, MA, USA).

Preprocessing consisted of realignment (seventh degree b-spline interpolation to the mean image), slice time correction (reference slice: first) and normalization to the standard space of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) EPI template (2 × 2 × 2 mm3 isotropic voxels, trilinear interpolation). Spatial smoothing was applied using an isotropic 3D Gaussian filter with a full width at half maximum of 8 mm to allow for corrected statistical inference.

For statistical analysis, on the first level, a fixed-effects general linear model was calculated separately for each subject and both paradigms. For both paradigms, the six movement parameters (three translations, three rotations) were used as covariates of no interest. Contrasts from this first-level analysis were introduced to the second-level random-effects analyses to test for effects on the population level.

For the reward paradigm, regressors of interest (win cue, verbal cue, control cue) were convolved with the default SPM hemodynamic response function computed as a 2-parameter gamma function. A high-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 1/128 Hz was used to reduce low frequency components. Analyses were corrected for serial-correlated errors by fitting a first-order autoregressive process (AR[1]) to the error term. To test for successful activation of the VS and potential group differences in striatal sensitivity, the contrast win cue > control cue was calculated and introduced to second-level mixed-effect models. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to test for the main effect of group. In addition, a one-sample t-test looking on all participants was calculated to test for the main effect of condition (win cue > control cue).

For the neurofeedback training, a block design was defined with feedback and rest (fixation cross) blocks and the sessions separately. Due to the long block length, the cut-off frequency of the high-pass filter was set to 1/256 Hz. Again, analyses were corrected for serial-correlated errors by fitting a first-order autoregressive process (AR[1]) to the error term.

Three contrasts of interest were defined for the neurofeedback condition: (1) reduction of activation during feedback over time by defining the interaction term (Feedback 1 − Rest 1) > (Rest 3 − Feedback 3) to identify brain regions that showed a reduction of activation over time; (2) increase of activation during feedback over time by defining the interaction term (Feedback 3 − Rest 3) > (Feedback 1 − Rest 1) to identify control region with increasing activation due to downregulation effort; and (3) generally increased activation during feedback compared to rest (Feedback 1 + Feedback 2 + Feedback 3) > (Rest 1 + Rest 2 + Rest 3) to identify regions with increased activation during downregulation effort. Again, the contrasts of interest were introduced to a second-level one-way ANOVA to compute the differences between the three groups.

The NF effect in the VS was tested using a ROI analysis with a sphere of 2 cm around the mean coordinate of the nucleus accumbens as reported in the Brede database (Nielsen 2003) (MNI coordinates 12, 12, −6).

Finally, to check for sustained effects of the neurofeedback training, the brain responses to the alcohol stimuli during the transfer block within the region showing a downregulation effect for the training were extracted for the rFB and the yFB groups. Differences in the activation in the regions found to be successfully downregulated during the training were tested by means of an independent sample t-test.

Visual analog craving scales

Before and after the scanning session, the participants were asked to rate five craving-related questions on a visual analog scale ranging from ‘0 strongly disagree‘ to ‘100 strongly agree’. This questionnaire included items on alcohol craving, negative mood, intention to drink, expected positive effect of alcohol and the desire not to drink. A craving sum score was calculated separately for both timepoints and compared for changes over time in a 3 (groups) × 2 (times) repeated measurement ANOVA with Bonferroni's corrected post hoc tests with the SPSS 22 software package (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Downregulation strategies

After the neurofeedback training, the participants of the rFB and the yFB groups were asked to report the strategies they used and to indicate whether the particular strategy was subjectively successful. The mean number of subjectively successful strategies was compared between groups by means of a two-sample t-test. In addition, the content of the reported strategies was qualitatively analyzed by six raters. The frequencies of contents were compared between the groups by means of a χ2 test.

Results

fMRI results

Reward paradigm

During the reward paradigm, all participants showed an increased activation of the VS when compared to the neutral condition {P < 0.05, familywise error [FWE] corrected for the entire brain [peak voxel: x = 6, y = 8, z = −6, T(37) = 6.49, P < 0.001 corr.]; Supporting Information Fig. S2}. There were no significant activation differences (at P < 0.001, uncorrected, k = 20) between groups reflecting comparable sensitivity of the reward system in all groups.

Neurofeedback training

There was a stronger decline of striatal activation to alcohol cues compared to rest over three training blocks in the rFB group compared with the two control groups [P < 0.05, FWE corrected for the ROI, peak voxel: x = 10, y = 26, z = −2, T(35) = 4.55, P = 0.01, cluster size = 99 voxels; Fig. 2, left]. When looking on the time course of activation separately for the three groups, we found a steady decrease only for the rFB group (Fig. 2, right).

When looking for regions with increased activation during feedback, we were not able to identify regions with a specific, feedback-related increase of activation over the three training sessions: there were no regions where the rFB group showed more increase of activation than the other groups. However, when looking on the general activation over the whole training session, we found a significantly stronger activation in the rFB group compared with the control groups in two clusters (P < 0.05, FWE corrected for the entire brain) in the right inferior frontal gyrus [peak voxel: x = 30, y = 20, z = 2, T(35) = 6.46, P < 0.005 corr.; Fig. 3a]. When analyzing the activation within the most significant cluster between groups, we found that the activation differed significantly between all three groups with the lowest activation in the noFB group and the highest activation in the rFB group (Fig. 3b).

Since we attributed this activation to cognitive control processes during downregulation, we correlated the mean activation in the most significant cluster with the amount of striatal downregulation. Interestingly, a significant correlation was exclusively identified for the rFB group [r = 0.46, P = 0.05 (one-sided); Fig. 3c].

Finally, we compared the response to the alcohol cues during the transfer block between the rFB and the yFB groups and found a significantly higher activation of the VS in the yFB group [T(24) = 1.91, P < 0.04, one-sided].

Subjective craving data

For the analysis, the five craving-related questions were averaged. We found a significant group × time interaction [F (2/32) = 4.137, P < 0.024]. While we found an increased subjective craving after the presentation of alcohol cues in the noFB group (post hoc test: P < 0.01), no increase or even a (non-significant) decrease of subjective craving was found for the rFB and the yFB groups (Fig. 4). There was no correlation between the subjective craving or its change over time and the ventral striatal activation in any of the groups.

Downregulation strategies

The participants of the rFB group reported significantly more subjectively successful strategies than the yFB group [2.85 (SD 1.4) versus 1.77 (SD 1.23), T(24) = 2.1, P < 0.05]. The content analysis identified four main clusters of downregulation strategies: (1) imagination of negative consequences of alcohol; (2) distraction with negative thoughts; (3) distraction with positive or neutral thoughts; and (4) mindful thoughts. The frequency of these content clusters did not differ between groups.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to explore the potential applicability of real-time neurofeedback to reduce striatal cue-reactivity to alcohol stimuli. Given the meta-analytic evidence for a robust alcohol cue-induced activation of striatal regions (Schacht et al. 2013) and the association of activation in these regions with relapse (Grusser et al. 2004) and craving (Myrick et al. 2004; Kuhn & Gallinat 2011), the VS appears as a reasonable target for neurofeedback training. However, before testing this approach in patients, we decided to conduct a proof-of-concept study in a group of heavy drinking individuals. The main result of this study is that the downregulation of striatal activation was successful. Only participants from the group receiving real neurofeedback were able to successfully downregulate the cue-induced activation of the striatum and to transfer that ability in a situation without feedback. This effect seems to be dependent on the feedback signal since the simple instruction to downregulate activation together with a feedback signal from another participant in the yNF group was not successful, although these participants also tried to downregulate their brain activation as reflected by significant prefrontal activation. In addition, for the second control group that was instructed to only view the pictures without doing any kind of regulation, no significant changes in the striatal activation across the feedback blocks were observed, ruling out habituation as a potential source of the effect in the rFB group. Therefore, our results strongly support our assumption that the striatal downregulation observed in the rFB group can be accounted as an active process depending on the real feedback. This, to our knowledge, first study demonstrating a successful modulation of the striatal cue-reactivity strongly supports the idea of using real-time fMRI neurofeedback to treat alcohol dependence.

However, looking on the analysis of brain activation that was stronger in the feedback compared with the rest blocks resulted in a slightly different picture. First of all, one could expect that we find an activation pattern that contrasts with what we saw in the VS, a training-associated increase of activation over the time course in prefrontal executive control structure for the rFB but not the control groups. However, we were not able to find such pattern. In contrast, we observe a generally increased activation in the right inferior frontal gyrus over all three sessions not only for the rFB group but also for the yFB group. Only the noFB group that was not instructed to conduct any kind of regulation showed no activation. The region activated during regulation attempts has been repeatedly associated with executive functions (EFs) (Alvarez & Emory 2006). This is very reasonable since EFs are known to be involved in planning, volition, purposive action and effective performance. Therefore, this increased right inferior frontal gyrus activation reflects the active and conscious process initiated by the participants of the active FB groups to downregulate the thermometer. However, this active and conscious process was observed over the whole training period and was not an effect of the training itself since it did not increase over time. The fact that the activation in the inferior frontal gyrus was correlated with the decrease of VS activation exclusively in the rFB group supports the assumption that this active effort of the participants was related to the NF training effect. However, obviously, the activation pattern cannot distinguish between successful and non-successful effort in the prefrontal control regions.

Volkow et al. (2004) proposed four strategies for interventions in addicted individuals, decreasing the reward value of the drug, increasing the saliency value of non-drug reinforcers, diminishing the conditioned drug responses and strengthening the inhibitory and executive control. According to our results, NF could be effective in two of these strategies. First, NF is reasonable to reduce the conditioned drug responses. The cue-reactivity of the VS, part of the reward system, was actively reduced. Second, the participants were guided to strengthen their executive control functions. However, according to the right inferior frontal gyrus activation pattern, the latter is also true for the yNF group, although in a slightly reduced amount. In addition, successful downregulation of the striatal region exclusively correlated with the right inferior frontal gyrus activation in the rFB group. This pattern suggests that the successful downregulation of the ventral striatal activation strengthens the executive control efforts reflected in the right inferior frontal gyrus, leading to sustainable and goal directed training effects. For future approaches, it could be reasonable to use feedback of the connectivity between prefrontal and striatal regions to directly train the modulatory effect of the PFC (Zilverstand et al. 2014).

In the field of addiction therapy, many treatment approaches used are based on distraction or avoidance strategies. Even exposure-based therapy (Vollstadt-Klein et al. 2011), based on the mechanism of extinction learning, can be accounted as a more passive approach. In contrast, NF is an active treatment, training the patients to gain voluntary control over their ventral striatal activation, or in clinical terms, over their craving. Therefore, NF could be a promising supplement to traditional addiction treatment where patients might learn that they are not passively condemned to respond to a drug cue in a particular way but have options to actively resist and reduce the desire and with that get back control over their own lives. In the terms of Bandura (1977), the experience of control could lead to enhancement of perceived self-efficacy, which has been identified as a very effective mechanism underlying therapy response. In the end, NF could develop an effect far beyond its simple biological mechanism changing the attitudes of patients and with that the long-term course of the disease. This increased self-efficacy is also reflected in the significant difference in successful downregulation strategies in the two groups. The participants in the rFB group experienced the success of their effort significantly more often than patients in the yFB group. However, it is an interesting result that even without a contingent feedback, in a free interview after scanning, many participants reported a subjective impression of control over the feedback signal assuming neurofeedback methods could even show some effect without a real feedback (Katz et al. 1987). This idea is also consistent with our data regarding subjective craving. Only participants in the noFB group, who did not try to regulate craving in any way, showed an effect of the presented alcohol cues leading to an increased subjective craving. This reflects the general ability of the pictures used to induce craving. In contrast to the noFB group, both active FB groups, where most participants reported efficient downregulation strategies, did not show any effect of the alcohol cues on the development of craving.

Although this study was very successful in applying rtfMRI NF to alcohol cue-associated brain activation, it has a number of shortcomings that should be considered in future studies. First of all, the approach to create the feedback mask is not completely satisfying. In this study, we used a functional localizer where we identified the striatal target region by means of a monetary reward paradigm. Although on a group level we were able to activate the VS, it is not clear whether our masks did really fit on an individual level. In addition, it is not clear whether the region activated by the anticipation of a monetary reward is exactly the same activated by an alcohol cue. Hence, for future studies, we would recommend using a combination of functional and anatomical localizer, particularly when more than one training day is planned. In addition, it could be challenged that we used a yoke control group. Other groups use ‘sham’ feedback from a region not involved in the process under investigation as control condition. Doing this could further disentangle the specific effects of the feedback from the target region and the effects of self-perceived efficacy. However, since in this exploratory pilot study we had no clues about additional regions involved in the specific downregulation process, we decided to use a non-contingent feedback from the target region rather than a contingent one from a control region. In this context, it is also important to ask whether the subjects were successfully blinded, i.e. whether they recognized the missing contingency between their brain activity and the feedback signal in the yFB group. Although we, unfortunately, have not asked systematically about their beliefs, we can suppose that the participants were successfully blinded. While no participant reported the impression that there was something wrong with the feedback, the members of the yFB group were often relieved when learning that they did not receive real feedback during debriefing.

Another problem with the presented data is that we did not find a correlation between subjective craving and VS activation, neither at the beginning nor at the end of the training. This is inconsistent with the reports in the literature (Kuhn & Gallinat 2011). However, we have to keep in mind that this study investigated a non-clinical group of heavy drinking students. The amount of craving cannot be compared to what can be observed in addicted individuals. A study of NF in alcohol patients is necessary to further reveal the relation between the change in VS activation to alcohol cues and subjective craving.

Finally, in contrast to other clinical applications, we used only one session of training. It is neither clear whether a prolonged training could be more effective nor whether alcohol-dependent patients would be able to achieve ventral striatal downregulation as fast as our high-functioning university students did. Therefore, for patient studies, we plan to extend the training to at least three sessions on three different days. A possible disadvantage of the real-time neurofeedback methodology is the latency between measurement of activation and presentation of the feedback which we were only able to reduce to 6 seconds for our study. While for the participant of our study, this was not a problem, it is not clear whether patients can adapt to this latency as well. However, because the BOLD response used for the feedback is very slow per se, this problem cannot be solved. Nevertheless, the results of the present study encourage us to test it in a patient study allowing us to further conclude about the applicability of the method for the treatment in alcohol addiction.

Taken together, the results of our pilot study strengthen our confidence to use this method as a new treatment option in the field of alcohol addiction. NF is suitable to reduce striatal activation to alcohol cues. In addition, by activating executive control mechanisms, it supports the experience of self-efficacy in downregulating subjective craving. Future research must show whether addicted patients will benefit from this new approach.
