Abstract

Endocrine signals such as ghrelin and leptin are known to modulate the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic system and, consequently, show associations with food and drug reward. In animal models, nicotine was demonstrated to reduce body weight by attenuating food intake and effects of leptin and ghrelin are partly modulated by nicotinic acetylcholine receptors which hint at potential interactions. However, the neuropharmacological modulation of endocrine signals by nicotine in healthy humans remains to be tested experimentally. We used functional magnetic resonance imaging to investigate food-cue reactivity after an overnight fast and following a caloric load (oral glucose tolerance test, OGTT) in 26 healthy normal-weight never-smokers. Moreover, we administered either nicotine (2 mg) or placebo gums using a randomized cross-over design and assessed blood plasma levels of ghrelin and leptin. During fasting, nicotine administration decreased correlations with ghrelin levels in the mesocorticolimbic system whereas correlations with leptin were increased. After the OGTT, nicotine increased the modulatory effects of ghrelin and leptin on food-cue reactivity, particularly in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and the amygdala. Critically, this led to an indirect modulation of the behavioral ‘appetizer effect’ (i.e. cue-induced increases in subjective appetite) by homeostatic feedback signals via food-cue reactivity in vmPFC. We conclude that nicotine enhances the effect of ghrelin and leptin in the valuation and relevance network which might, in turn, reduce appetite. This highlights that amplifying the impact of homeostatic signals such as ghrelin and leptin in normal-weight individuals might hint at a mechanism contributing to nicotine's anorexic potential.

Introduction

Nowadays, populations in industrialized countries are confronted with a plethora of options that promise immediate pleasure, e.g. energy-dense fast food and legal drugs such as tobacco. Consequently, in the Untied States, roughly one-third of the adult population is considered obese (Flegal et al. 2012) and almost every fifth adult is a current cigarette smoker (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2011). Whereas the prevalence of obesity is somewhat lower in Europe (Berghöfer et al. 2008), smoking is more prevalent and approximately one-third of the population are current smokers in Germany (Baumeister et al. 2008). Moreover, smoking is well known to be associated with lower body weight and smoking cessation can lead to considerable weight gain (Aubin et al. 2012). However, variance in weight gain after smoking cessation is high: Whereas 16 percent of smokers even lose weight, 13 percent gain more than 10 kg (Aubin et al. 2012). Specifically, overweight and obese smokers gain more weight than normal-weight smokers (Lycett et al. 2011), which suggests that nicotine might have compensatory effects on body weight in individuals with a high risk for obesity and overeating. Of note, overweight and obesity seem to be characterized by insensitivity to leptin (indicated by central leptin deficiency due to altered blood-brain transport; Adam & Findlay 2010) and oversensitivity to ghrelin signaling (cf. Klok, Jakobsdottir & Drent 2007) and differential responses could help explain why certain individuals overeat (Small 2009). Moreover, decreases in smoking prevalence in developed countries were associated with increases in prevalence of obesity and might contribute to the current obesity epidemic (Flegal & Troiano 1995). Nevertheless, the neuropharmacological mechanism behind the interactions of nicotine and endocrine signaling remains to be addressed experimentally in healthy (i.e. normal-weight and non-dependent) individuals.

In obesogenic environments of industrialized countries, eating is often elicited by the availability of palatable food. Eating and food-cue reactivity are affected by a multitude of signaling agents and their complex interactions, particularly in the hypothalamus where many receptors for neuropeptides are primarily expressed. For example, ghrelin is a stomach-derived hormone (Kojima et al. 1999) which is the only known circulating peptide stimulating appetite by facilitating the production of neuropeptide Y (NPY) and agouti-related protein (AGRP; Arora 2006). Whereas effects of NPY are short lived, intracerebroventricular injections of AGRP lead to prolonged increases in energy intake which are attributable to selective antagonistic effects on melanocortin(MC)-3 and MC-4 receptors (cf. Arora 2006). Given this regulatory cascade, it is not surprising that ghrelin provides information on a short- (i.e. facilitating meal initiation; Cummings et al. 2001) and long-term scale [i.e. ghrelin levels correlate with body mass index (BMI); cf. Kroemer et al. 2012]. Leptin is less sensitive to short-term effects because it is produced by adipose tissue and, thereby, signals energy stores by encoding a cumulative energy balance (Chin-Chance, Polonsky & Schoeller 2000). It regulates food intake mainly via the long form of the leptin receptor (LepRB) which is densely expressed in the mediobasal hypothalamic ‘satiety’ (arcuate nucleus) and ‘feeding’ (lateral hypothalamic area) centers (Leinninger et al. 2009). In addition, the mesolimbic dopaminergic system (i.e. the ventral tegmental area, VTA, and the nucleus accumbens, NAcc) expresses receptors for ghrelin and leptin indicating the potential of both peptides to interact with reward processing in general. It is well known that leptin reduces food intake, in particular of highly palatable food, although mesolimbic dopamine release is not unidirectionally mirroring these effects (e.g. Fulton et al. 2006; Hommel et al. 2006; Leinninger et al. 2009).

In contrast to AGRP, activation of pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons has a complementary effect decreasing food intake via hypothalamic MC-receptor signaling. Leptin inhibits AGRP release and increases firing rates of a subpopulation of POMC neurons in the arcuate nucleus (Breen, Conwell & Wardlaw 2005). POMC neurons that express LepRB project to the paraventricular nucleus, the lateral hypothalamus, the NAcc and the amygdala (cf. Millington 2007). Intriguingly, nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) seem to modulate this circuit (Meister et al. 2006; Picciotto & Mineur 2013) and nicotine has been shown to activate POMC neurons, similar to leptin, but via α3β4 nAChRs in rodents. Moreover, anorexic effects of nicotine are attributable to POMC projections to second-order MC neurons in the paraventricular nucleus and this signaling cascade is sufficient and necessary for nicotine to inhibit food intake effectively (Mineur et al. 2011). Recently, we have shown that nicotine administration in humans affects hypothalamic signaling in response to food pictures by decoupling the hedonic-hunger network from homeostatic hunger feedback (Kroemer et al. 2013a). Taken together, these results suggest that endogenous acetylcholine or nicotine may mediate the effect of leptin in this circuit (Meister et al. 2006).

However, nicotine has effects on other peptides as well and some of them should even counteract the anorexic potential. First, nicotine was shown to increase NPY expression in the hypothalamus (Li et al. 2000) while it reduces leptin levels leading to less food intake in total (Li & Kane 2003). Nevertheless, nicotine increased mRNA expression of OB-R leptin receptors and leptin binding, most notably in the hypothalamus and the piriform cortex (Li & Kane 2003). Second, smoking acutely increases ghrelin levels in smokers (Fagerberg, Hultén & Hulthe 2003; Bouros et al. 2006; Kokkinos et al. 2007) but decreases ghrelin levels in non-smokers (Kokkinos et al. 2007). Critically, cholinergic afferents to the VTA have been shown to be activated by ghrelin leading to dopamine overflow in the NAcc and this effect is blocked by the non-selective nicotinic antagonist mecamylamine (Jerlhag et al. 2006). In addition, mecamylamine blocks fasting- and ghrelin-induced food intake and reduces the ability of palatable food to condition a place preference (Dickson et al. 2010). In a similar vein, a ghrelin antagonist for the growth hormone secretagogue receptor 1A attenuates the rewarding effects of nicotine (Jerlhag & Engel 2011).

In conclusion, both endocrine signals and nicotine modulate the circuit that links dopamine and feeding to produce the well-known consequences on eating behavior but the exact mechanisms are largely unknown, specifically in humans. Moreover, signaling in the mesocorticolimbic system is modulated directly by different subtypes of nAChR (for a review, see Changeux 2010) which might impact on food-cue reactivity. Critically, systemic effects of nicotine (e.g. increases in metabolic rate) have been hypothesized to contribute to the anorexic effect of nicotine, but the current evidence does not support the conclusion that it has a substantial net effect on body weight (cf. Kroemer et al. 2013a).

Several imaging studies have demonstrated that homeostatic feeding signals such as leptin and ghrelin modulate food-cue reactivity as measured using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Employing an elegant study design, Rosenbaum et al. (2008) investigated six obese inpatients prior to weight loss and after stabilization of weight loss (approximately 10 percent) using placebo-controlled leptin repletion following a randomized cross-over design. Critically, leptin reversed decreased food-cue reactivity after weight loss in the hypothalamus, cingulate gyrus and middle frontal gyrus while reversing the increased food-cue reactivity, e.g. in the brainstem, culmen, parahippocampal gyrus, and inferior and middle frontal gyri. This suggests that changes in food-cue reactivity that might help to reinstate food-seeking behavior could potentially be reversed by leptin repletion (Rosenbaum et al. 2008). Intriguingly, obese patients showed enhanced food-cue reactivity in the putamen after leptin repletion and a recent imaging study showed that BMI and leptin levels are positively correlated with food-cue reactivity in the ventral striatum (Grosshans et al. 2012). This direction is surprising at first glance because leptin is known to reduce food intake and enhanced food-cue reactivity is associated with reduced short- and long-term weight loss outcomes (Murdaugh et al. 2012) and higher transitional increases in BMI (Demos, Heatherton & Kelley 2012). However, one reason might be that leptin regulation in obese participants is reversed due to insensitivity or central deficiency. Moreover, smokers (who were included in the study by Grosshans et al.) might show a different pattern because of increased binding of leptin (Li & Kane 2003). Another alternative is, again, that dopamine release itself has an anorexic effect because coadministration of D1- and D2-receptor agonists reduced food intake in rodents which strikingly resembles modulation by leptin (Kuo 2002). In line with this interpretation, dopamine D2-receptor blockade was shown to block the hypophagic action of leptin administration (Billes, Simonds & Cowley 2012).

With regard to ghrelin, the pattern is more conclusive. Malik et al. (2008) demonstrated that administration of ghrelin increased food-cue reactivity in regions that are involved in hedonic- and homeostatic hunger networks (van der Laan et al. 2011) such as the fusiform gyrus, the orbitofrontal cortex and the amygdala. We have extended this finding recently by demonstrating that food cue-reactivity, particularly in the hypothalamus, amygdala and the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, is correlated with endogenous fasting levels of ghrelin as well (Kroemer et al. 2012). This enhanced responsivity was echoed in an increased ‘appetizer effect’ (Yeomans 1996), i.e. higher fasting levels of ghrelin were associated with stronger increases in subjective appetite ratings during the presentation of food cues (Kroemer et al. 2012).

To summarize, although effects of certain neuromodulators such as leptin, ghrelin and nicotine are an active topic in current research, little is known about the interaction of different endocrine and pharmacological factors that ultimately give rise to complex eating behavior in humans. For example, nicotine reduces body weight by reducing food intake which is in turn modulated by complex interactions of nicotine with endocrine signals. Therefore, we investigated the acute effects of nicotine on the circulating peptides leptin and ghrelin by administering either 2 mg nicotine or placebo gums following a randomized cross-over design. We hypothesized that nicotine increases the modulatory (anorexic) effect of leptin while attenuating the modulatory (orexigenic) effect of fasting levels of ghrelin in the mesocorticolimbic system. Moreover, we expected that nicotine leads to larger post-load decreases of ghrelin or of brain responses correlated with ghrelin. To evaluate these effects, we combined endocrine data collected from blood samples reflecting homeostatic feedback (leptin, ghrelin), subjective appetite ratings during the paradigm reflecting cue-induced appetite, and food-cue reactivity using fMRI.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The present study is based on a sample reported in detail in a previous study (Kroemer et al. 2013b). For the following analyses, we analyzed 26 right-handed healthy never-smokers (13 female) with complete data. The current analyses focus on the modulation of homeostatic feedback information by nicotine which has not been published previously. Never-smokers were defined as current non-smokers who smoked less than 20 cigarettes in their lifetime. Their mean age was 24.4 ± 3.4 years and they were within the range of normal weight (i.e. 18.5 ≤ BMIi ≤ 24.9 kg/m2; MBMI = 21.1 ± 2.0 kg/m2). Notwithstanding, chronic administration of nicotine likely leads to different effects than a single acute dose of nicotine but the latter method provides an opportunity to study initial drug effects that may, in turn, influence future consumption of the substance. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the Faculty of Medicine Mannheim and informed consent was obtained from all participants (see Supporting Information).

Procedure

Participants arrived between 7:30 and 9:00 a.m. They were allowed to take the last meal at 10:00 p.m. the day before the experiment. Ten minutes after their arrival, the first blood sample was collected and participants were instructed to chew the nicotine (2 mg Nicorette®, Pfizer, New York City, NY, USA) versus placebo chewing gum for 30 minutes. After they finished chewing, a second blood sample was collected to assess nicotine levels. The stimulus set consisted of 120 food and 120 low-level control pictures. Food stimuli were selected based on high-palatability ratings in an evaluation study and control pictures were created by scrambling them in order to match in intensity, contrast and brightness. The fMRI session consisted of two 15-minute runs separated by a 5-minute break to administer the oral glucose tolerance test. In each run, pictures of both categories were presented in a block design with 12 food and control blocks in a pseudorandom order to ensure that three blocks in a row never contained the same stimulus category. Each block consisted of five pictures. Every picture was presented for 4 seconds via MR-compatible goggles using MRI Audio/Video Systems (Resonance Technology Inc., Northridge, CA, USA).

After presenting the last picture of a block, a fixation cross appeared for 500 ms. Thereafter, a rating slide with the statement ‘I feel like eating now’ was presented. Participants had to indicate how much they felt like eating at the moment on a visual analog scale with the end-points: ‘totally disagree’ versus ‘totally agree’ by moving an MR-compatible response pad with their right hands. A fixation cross followed and after a total time of 15.5 seconds, a new block started. After the first run, participants were removed from the scanner and drank a solution containing the equivalent of 75 g of glucose (defined by a 300 ml mixture of mono- and oligosaccharides; ACCU-CHEK®Dextro® O.G-T., Roche, Basel, Switzerland) within 5 minutes while lying on the scanner bed. Five minutes after they started drinking, participants continued with the second fMRI run, which consisted of new pictures presented in the same design. Presentation® software (Version 9.90, Neurobehavioral Systems Inc., Albany, CA, USA) was used to present pictures and collect data.

Data acquisition and analysis

Images were acquired with a 3 Tesla whole-body MRI scanner (TRIO, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 12-channel head coil. After preprocessing with SPM5, first-level contrast images food–control pictures (see Supporting Information) were entered into a second-level analysis comprising a repeated measures ANOVA. First, the fasting state models used only data from the first run and consisted of the two-level within-subject factor nicotine (2 mg versus placebo) including ghrelin and leptin (for sample treatment and hormone measurements, see Supporting Information) as covariate that interacted with the factor nicotine, respectively. As described in Kroemer et al. (2012), we averaged the first three ghrelin samples to robustly estimate fasting levels. We did not use post-load decreases of ghrelin levels because they were highly correlated with fasting levels and, thus, did not provide additional variance information. To remove sex differences in blood plasma levels of both hormones, we centered the regressors separately for women and men before entering them. This is particularly important for leptin because of the high correlation of blood levels with sex (>.70; see Results). Without removing the mean difference between sexes, correlations could be hugely driven by this confounding factor. Second, the caloric state models used the contrast images Run2(food–control)—Run1(food–control) and consisted again of the two-level within-subject factor nicotine (2 mg versus placebo) including ghrelin and leptin as covariate that interacted with the factor nicotine, respectively.

We expected nicotine administration to modulate the correlation of ghrelin and leptin with food-cue reactivity in regions that are critically involved in food reward and express receptors [i.e. hypothalamus, VTA, NAcc, amygdala, ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and dorsal striatum; for information on the regions of interest (ROIs), see Supporting Information]. To compare model fit in placebo and nicotine sessions, we used extracted signal change for the contrast food–control pictures (‘SPM betas’) from ROIs. We set up two multi-level analyses using HLM7 (Raudenbush et al. 2011) and defined the fasting scan as intercept and the change to the post-load run as slope (fixed; baseline model) which were both modeled by leptin and ghrelin as second-level predictor variables (i.e. leptin + ghrelin model) for nicotine and placebo sessions separately. We computed the deviance statistic for the baseline model with four free parameters and the leptin + ghrelin model (eight free parameters) using full maximum likelihood estimation and computed the difference in scores according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the Akaike information criterion (AIC) 1. Please note that this analysis is complementary in the sense that model fit analyses do not take the sign into account but rather reflect if a certain model is a better predictor of the data (i.e. minimizes the model residuals).

Furthermore, we used restricted maximum likelihood estimation as implemented in HLM7 to derive estimates for initial appetite ratings (intercepts at block 0) and the behavioral appetizer effect, i.e. increases in subjective appetite in response to the food pictures (slopes; for details, see Kroemer et al. 2012). This analysis allows for separation of the effects of homeostatic hunger (intercepts) and cue-induced appetite (slopes; i.e. changes over blocks) that are known to have distinct psychological and neural correlates (cf. Kroemer et al. 2013b). In addition, we explored whole-brain differences using an uncorrected P < .001 with a minimal cluster size k = 20 as primary statistical threshold because it is sufficiently balanced regarding Type I and Type II errors (Lieberman & Cunningham 2009). To facilitate visualization of clusters, we show P < .005, uncorrected, in our figures as well, but do not rely on this threshold for inference. Nevertheless, we report information on family-wise error (FWE) whole-brain correction for intensity and extent (based on Monte Carlo simulations, AlphaSim, or random field theory). For analyses of behavioral and ROI data, we used a threshold of P < .05 (two tailed). For further statistical analyses, we used SPSS 19 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Endocrinological data

Nicotine administration did not significantly affect ghrelin levels (Fig. 1). However, nicotine tended to decrease ghrelin levels more strongly as a function of time (repeated measures ANOVA, multivariate Nicotine × Time; F(6,19) = 2.415; P = .066). Nevertheless, sex centering removed the difference in fasting ghrelin effectively and the correlation between nicotine and placebo sessions was .89 (ρ = .88; Ps < .001). Leptin levels were assessed before administration of nicotine and were highly correlated as well (r = .70; ρ = .69; Ps < .001).

Subjective appetite ratings

In general, administration of nicotine had a small- to medium-sized attenuating effect on subjective appetite ratings during fasting but not after caloric load (for details, see Kroemer et al. 2013a). However, there were no significant differences in initial subjective appetite ratings (i.e. the intercept at block 0) and the behavioral appetizer effect (i.e. increases in subjective appetite ratings in response to food-cue exposure over blocks; ts < 1) attributable to nicotine. In regression analyses using initial appetite ratings or the appetizer effect as criteria, there were no significant associations if both leptin and ghrelin were entered as predictors.

Imaging data

Main effect of nicotine: fasting state

During the fasting state, administration of nicotine reduced food-cue reactivity in the right hypothalamus. In contrast, activation increased in the middle temporal gyrus (BA22; −50/−44/−4; tmax = 3.98), middle frontal gyrus (BA6; 34/12/50; tmax = 3.88) and the culmen (−38/−56/−36; tmax = 3.88; for main effects of nicotine, see Kroemer et al. 2013a).

Modulated food-cue reactivity: correlations with ghrelin during fasting state

Administration of nicotine reduced the correlation with fasting levels of ghrelin in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system (Fig. 2a). Particularly, nicotine tended to modulate the correlation with fasting ghrelin in our a priori ROIs NAcc, amygdala and hypothalamus (Table 1). In addition, we compared the correlation with the bilaterally averaged food-cue reactivity using a multi-level regression model (see Supporting Information). This indicated that nicotine significantly reduced the average correlation in NAcc (t = −2.14; P = .043) and amygdala (t = −2.50; P = .020), but not in the hypothalamus (t = −1.45; P = .16). Moreover, it reduced the correlation with fasting ghrelin in the periaqueductal gray (Supporting Information Table S1). All other ROIs showed similar trends but failed to exceed FWE-corrected significance levels.

Modulated food-cue reactivity: correlations with leptin during fasting state

Administration of nicotine increased the correlation with fasting levels of leptin, most notably in the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic system, the somatosensory and motor cortices, the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the periaqueductal gray (PAG; Fig. 2b, Supporting Information Table S1). Particularly, nicotine modulated the correlation with leptin in all our a priori ROIs of vmPFC, NAcc, dorsal striatum, amygdala, VTA and hypothalamus (Table 1).

Modulated food-cue reactivity: correlations with ghrelin and interaction with caloric load

After caloric load, changes in food-cue reactivity were positively correlated with fasting ghrelin (regardless of the factor nicotine) and located mainly within our a priori ROIs of NAcc (left: −8/14/−12; tmax = 2.62, PFWE = .23; right: 12/4/−12 tmax = 3.77, PFWE = .019) and amygdala (left: −26/0/−26; tmax = 3.73, PFWE = .024; right: 24/−6/−16; tmax = 2.79, PFWE = .19). The right hypothalamus (tmax = 2.88, PFWE = .058) and the vmPFC (4/28/−14; tmax = 3.15, PFWE = .064) showed a tendency in the same direction which were, however, not significant after FWE correction (for clusters exceeding our exploratory whole-brain threshold, see Supporting Information Table S2).

Nicotine tended to amplify the positive correlation in vmPFC (0/28/−6; tmax = 3.21, PFWE = .055; Fig. 3). Because this finding was close to significance, we assessed the difference in correlation coefficients between nicotine and placebo sessions using confidence intervals for non-overlapping dependent correlations (Zou 2007). The 95% CI was rdiff = [−.03, .92] whereas the 90% CI was rdiff = [.05, .86]. Of note, a sensitivity power analysis (α = .05) indicated that to detect (1 − β = .50) a significantly higher correlation given H0 = .11, a correlation must exceed .61 whereas it must exceed .75 to give reasonable power (1 − β = .80). Thus, our result provides some evidence for modulation by nicotine and the large uncertainty might argue for a lower threshold to balance Type I and Type II errors but, ultimately, larger samples will be needed to verify this finding.

Modulated food-cue reactivity: correlations with leptin and interaction with caloric load

In general, changes in food-cue reactivity after caloric load were negatively correlated with leptin (regardless of the factor nicotine administration) in middle temporal gyrus (BA21; 68/−26/−8; tmax = 4.15), thalamus (anterior nucleus; −8/−8/18; tmax = 3.96), inferior parietal lobule (BA40; 52/−62/38; tmax = 3.95), midbrain (4/−28/−20; tmax = 3.93) and orbitofrontal cortex (BA10; −28/52/−6; tmax = 3.86). Moreover, there was a significantly negative correlation within our a priori ROI amygdala (−30/0/−22; tmax = 3.56; PFWE = .036). No positive correlations with leptin survived the exploratory threshold (Fig. 4a).

Notably, nicotine administration interacted with caloric load in modulating the effects of leptin on food-cue reactivity. Whereas correlations with leptin increased after caloric load during placebo sessions in striatum, vmPFC, dmPFC/ACC (anterior cingulate cortex), mid cingulum, somatosensory system and motor cortices, they decreased after nicotine administration (Fig. 4). However, peak voxels in vmPFC and ventral striatum did not survive FWE correction (Table 1; Supporting Information Table S2). Again, we used a multi-level regression model (see Supporting Information) to test the difference between changes in food-cue reactivity averaged within the a priori ROIs. This indicated that nicotine significantly reduced the average correlation of leptin with changes of food-cue reactivity in vmPFC (t = −2.17; P = .041), but not in bilateral NAcc (t = −1.14; P = .27).

Psychophysiological interaction: nicotine decreases the correlation of ghrelin with functional coupling of hypothalamus and vmPFC

As we have outlined in the Introduction, the hypothalamus is hypothesized to be the key region with regard to interactions of nicotine, ghrelin and leptin. We reassessed the psychophysiological interaction (PPI) statistics described in Kroemer et al. (2013a; for details, see Supporting Information) that used a right hypothalamic cluster (10/0/−4) as seed region and extracted the PPI estimates of functional coupling to vmPFC. Regression analyses using ghrelin and leptin levels as predictors indicated that during placebo sessions, the functional coupling of hypothalamus and vmPFC was positively correlated with ghrelin (βPla = .35) but negatively during nicotine sessions (βNic = −.26), and this was significantly different as indicated by the interaction term Ghrelin × Nicotine Administration in a multi-level regression analysis (t = −2.68, P = .01; see Supporting Information). Leptin showed no significant main or interaction effect. This pattern was reflected in the association with the behavioral appetizer effect as well: Higher PPI estimates tended to correlate positively with the appetizer effect during placebo sessions (r = .30), whereas it tended to be negative during nicotine sessions (r = −.15). However, this difference was not significant within the multi-level regression analysis (t = −1.70; P = .10).

ROI model comparisons: nicotine increases modulation of vmPFC and amygdala by leptin and ghrelin

We have reported before converging evidence that nicotine modulates the correspondence between food cue-reactivity in the vmPFC and fasting levels of ghrelin and leptin. However, this does not necessarily indicate that food-cue reactivity in the vmPFC was more strongly modulated by ghrelin and leptin during nicotine sessions which we addressed in an analysis of our a priori ROI data that assessed model fit indices (Table 2; Fig. 5). During nicotine sessions, the BIC difference scores indicated that the leptin + ghrelin model fitted the data better than during placebo sessions for vmPFC (ΔBIC.Nic-Pla = 18.9) and amygdala (ΔBIC.Nic-Pla = 12.1). In all other ROIs, the difference between placebo and nicotine sessions was below 5. BIC scores indicated that the four additional parameters in the leptin + ghrelin model (given the cost in parsimony) improved fit in vmPFC (ΔAIC.Nic = 12.9; ΔBIC.Nic = 7.9) and amygdala (ΔAIC.Nic = 6.7; ΔBIC.Nic = 1.7), and the difference was more pronounced in AIC scores. However, the comparison of placebo versus nicotine sessions is not affected by the choice of model comparison score.

ROI path analysis: nicotine affects modulation of the appetizer affect via vmPFC signaling

Given the result that vmPFC signaling, particularly changes after caloric load, is more strongly modulated by leptin and ghrelin during nicotine sessions, we sought to assess how this corresponds to the appetizer effect which quantifies the appetite-inducing effect of food pictures. Notably, path analyses indicated a change in the signaling cascade. During placebo sessions, none of the paths were significant. During nicotine sessions, however, leptin (P = .042; 95% CI [−.01, −.57]) and ghrelin (P = .041; 95% CI [.01, .59]) both had a significant indirect effect on the appetizer effect via vmPFC slope (i.e. change after caloric load) in the hypothesized direction. This illustrates that nicotine administration led to full mediation of the appetizer effect through vmPFC food-cue reactivity.

Discussion

The current data demonstrated that nicotine had a modulatory effect on food-cue reactivity correlated with leptin and ghrelin during fasting and alterations by caloric load. In accordance with our predictions, nicotine attenuated correlations with fasting levels of ghrelin during the fasting scan in ventral striatum, amygdala and PAG. Moreover, nicotine increased correlations with fasting levels of leptin during the fasting scan in ventral and dorsal striatum, vmPFC and dmPFC, amygdala, hypothalamus, VTA, lateral orbitofrontal cortex (lOFC), somatosensory and motor cortices, and PAG. Given that overweight and obesity are associated with reduced sensitivity to leptin and oversensitivity to ghrelin, nicotine might be more effective in high-BMI individuals because it opposes these adaptations, but this remains to be tested in an obese sample. Critically, nicotine led to indirect modulation of the appetizer effect via food-cue reactivity in vmPFC. Notably, this correspondence was evident in a passive viewing task (i.e. cue-reactivity paradigm measuring anticipation), but it is mandatory to extend future research to include different aspects of food behavior such as consumption or dietary choice to strengthen this association with regard to ingestive behavior.

After caloric load, correlations of ghrelin with food-cue reactivity increased in amygdala, hypothalamus/ventral pallidum, and vmPFC. This modulatory effect in vmPFC was stronger during nicotine sessions. With regard to leptin, caloric load decreased the correlation in midbrain, amygdala and lOFC. Nicotine reversed the pattern, however, in many brain regions such as vmPFC, dmPFC/ACC, ventral striatum, and the somatosensory and motor cortices. A priori ROI analyses indicated that nicotine increased the modulatory effect of nicotine in vmPFC and amygdala. Furthermore, path analyses demonstrated that during nicotine sessions, the vmPFC signal change in response to caloric load mediated the effects of ghrelin and leptin on the appetizer effect (i.e. a significant indirect effect).

One consistent finding was that nicotine administration affected the correlations of leptin and ghrelin with food-cue reactivity in vmPFC. Nicotine increased the correlation of food-cue reactivity with leptin during fasting. Moreover, caloric load increased the correlation with ghrelin and this effect was stronger during nicotine sessions. In contrast, nicotine administration reduced the correlation with leptin after caloric load. The vmPFC is well known to forward a valuation signal representing goal (or expected outcome) values on a common scale in general (Grabenhorst & Rolls 2011; Levy & Glimcher 2012) and specifically with regard to food reward cues (Hare, Camerer & Rangel 2009). This signal guides reward-dependent decision making by transforming outcome differences of potential actions to consequent choices (Noonan et al. 2010; Grabenhorst & Rolls 2011; Levy & Glimcher 2012; cf. Rushworth et al. 2011). Whereas there is an ongoing discussion if vmPFC generates an automatic valuation signal (Rushworth et al. 2011), our subjective appetite rating after every food block might have implicitly introduced evaluative aspects. The differential pattern of fasting ghrelin and leptin suggests that nicotine increases dependency of the vmPFC valuation signal on metabolic state and related neuroendocrine signals that modulate the dopaminergic afferents from VTA. Consequently, model comparisons indicated that leptin and fasting ghrelin regressors explained more variance in vmPFC signaling during nicotine sessions. Critically, path analyses showed that the appetizer effect was dependent on the effect of caloric load on vmPFC signaling during nicotine sessions only which was, in turn, now affected by leptin and ghrelin. Taken together, this supports the conclusion that nicotine increases neuroendocrine coupling of the vmPFC valuation signal which might affect food choice behavior.

Whereas vmPFC is sometimes referred to as part of the salience network as well (cf. Rushworth et al. 2011), nicotine administration affected food-cue reactivity in the amygdala that is commonly associated with salience. Recent theories have expanded the functional label of the amygdala from emotional processing to salience or relevance in general (Ousdal et al. 2012). The amygdala was meta-analytically shown to be more responsive to food cues during a hungry than a satiated state (van der Laan et al. 2011) echoing the hypothesized relevance dimension. In our study, nicotine administration increased the correlation with leptin during the fasting state. Moreover, caloric load led to changes in the amygdala that were positively modulated by ghrelin (particularly after nicotine administration). Our model comparisons indicated that leptin and ghrelin modulated food-cue reactivity in the amygdala more strongly during nicotine sessions. Although the BIC score did not indicate a strong preference for the four- or eight-parameter model, AIC values would have clearly favored the more complex model. This indicates that for nicotine sessions, food-cue reactivity in the amygdala was more dependent on leptin during fasting whereas the effect of caloric intake was more dependent on ghrelin levels (similar to vmPFC). This pattern is well in line with the hypothesized function of leptin (i.e. encoding energy stores) and ghrelin (i.e. encoding motivational salience of food reward), and suggests that nicotine increases the neuroendocrine coupling of the salience/relevance signal forwarded by the amygdala.

Regarding whole-brain differences in the modulatory effects of nicotine on food-cue reactivity associated with leptin and ghrelin, it is apparent that the latter was more strongly restricted to the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic pathways and the ventromedial part of the frontal lobe (even if the threshold was lowered; results not reported). Whereas the modulatory effect of nicotine on leptin appeared stronger overall, there was a striking difference in dmPFC and motor and somatosensory cortices. The latter two regions are known to contain LepRB receptors in mice (Scott et al. 2009). Our findings might suggest that nicotine modulates locally unspecific the correlations of leptin and ghrelin and that leptin provides information on energy stores more generally within the cortex (i.e. beyond the mesocorticolimbic system). Interestingly, nicotine's direct effects on food-cue reactivity were locally restricted to a cluster that extended from the hypothalamus to the globus pallidus and ventral striatum which showed attenuated food-cue reactivity after caloric intake during placebo (Kroemer et al. 2013b), and PPI results suggested that this had functional consequences on food processing networks (Kroemer et al. 2013a). Thus, we hypothesize that whole-brain effects of nicotine on food processing occur by direct modulation of nicotinergic α3β4 POMC neurons in the hypothalamus. Neuroendocrine signaling in the hypothalamus, in turn, may support transcoding these local changes of neural activation into spatially extended firing patterns that ultimately lead to less food intake behaviorally. Hence, our findings do not contradict the conclusion that during fasting, nicotine reduces input of the homeostatic network on hedonic processing. In fact, it seems that food-cue reactivity in vmPFC and amygdala is now regulated by ghrelin and leptin more directly and less via hypothalamic input. Given the results of Billes et al. (2012), an interference with the hypothalamic pathway of ghrelin signaling induced by nicotine might attenuate the facilitating effect on food-cue reactivity and appetite. Our PPI analyses support the basic idea by demonstrating that nicotine reduces the correlation of ghrelin with functional coupling between hypothalamus and vmPFC, but further research is needed to investigate these complex interactions.

A major limitation of the current study is the fact that we have investigated only participants within the normal range of BMI. Thus, extrapolation to overweight and obese individuals is necessarily limited. Clearly, it is desirable to repeat the current study including a more diverse range of participants in terms of BMI. Second, we only investigated food-cue reactivity and cue-induced appetite. As we have outlined before, we hypothesize that modulatory effects of nicotine on leptin and ghrelin signaling may affect food reward decisions more strongly compared with mere food-cue reactivity. Thus, a future study should test this prediction explicitly, e.g. using a paradigm similar to Hare et al. (2009). Third, acute administration of nicotine only resembles specific characteristics that add to the complex effects of smoking on body weight. Ideally, our analyses would be repeated using a sample of quitting smokers following a pre-post design to improve generalization. Fourth, modulatory effects of caloric load might be different if a full meal was administered instead because some of the interaction effects of nicotine might be attributable to altered glucose sensitivity (Morgan et al. 2004).

To summarize, during fasting nicotine decreased correlations with ghrelin levels in the mesocorticolimbic system whereas correlations with leptin were increased. After caloric load, nicotine increased the modulatory effects of ghrelin and leptin on food-cue reactivity, particularly in vmPFC and amygdala. Critically, this also affected the regulatory cascade between fasting ghrelin and leptin, food-cue reactivity in the vmPFC, and the behavioral appetizer effect (i.e. cue-induced increases in subjective appetite). In conclusion, nicotine enhances the effect of ghrelin and leptin in the valuation (i.e. vmPFC) and salience network (i.e. amygdala). Whereas activation in the amygdala is thought to entail emotional aspects of hunger (i.e. a metabolic state of high relevance), vmPFC encodes the goal values of reward options and food items in particular (Hare et al. 2009). Consequently, this was echoed in nicotine-dependent mediation of subjective appetite through vmPFC by the dopaminergic neuromodulators leptin and ghrelin. Ultimately, this might help understand why individuals with overweight or obesity show stronger effects of nicotine on body weight because higher BMI is associated with desensitivity to leptin and oversensitivity to ghrelin. Hence, nicotine might improve decision making in being confronted with palatable food in an environment of abundance because it increases the impact of homeostatic signals on processing of available options in vmPFC and amygdala.
