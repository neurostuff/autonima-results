Abstract

Ghrelin has been shown to be involved in the pathophysiology of alcohol dependence, affecting alcohol self-administration and craving. However, the mechanism of action in alcohol dependence still has to be determined. We thus investigated whether ghrelin is associated with mesolimbic cue reactivity to alcohol cues and alcohol craving in recently detoxified alcohol-addicted subjects. We included 41 recently detoxified alcohol-dependent individuals. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) was used to study mesolimbic cue reactivity during the presentation of alcohol-related pictures. Additionally, we assessed patients' alcohol craving using the Alcohol Urge Questionnaire and a visual analogue scale. Plasma concentrations of total and acylated (activated) ghrelin were measured in parallel to the fMRI session. The association between ghrelin plasma concentrations, mesolimbic cue reactivity and alcohol craving was assessed by performing correlation and mediation analyses. Alcohol-induced brain response in a network of brain clusters, including the right and left ventral striatum, showed a significant positive association with acylated ghrelin plasma concentration. Additionally, acylated ghrelin was significantly associated with craving. Mediation analyses showed that the association between acylated ghrelin plasma concentration and alcohol craving is mediated by a cue-induced brain response in the ventral striatum. Based on the finding that ghrelin modulates mesolimbic reactivity to alcohol cues, the following should be considered: If alcohol craving and the appetitive status were interrelated, this has to be taken into account when implementing fMRI studies for addictive disorders. Moreover, appetite regulation seems to represent a valid treatment target for reducing cue reactivity in addictive disorders.

Introduction

In the context of appetite regulation, ghrelin was the first identified circulating orexigenic hormone (Arora and Anubhuti 2006). In humans, 80 percent of the total amount of ghrelin is synthesized and secreted by the entero-endocrine cells of the stomach and the remaining 20 percent is synthesized and secreted by the entero-endocrine cells of the intestine and other tissues. In general, ghrelin is secreted as the precursor protein preproghrelin (Kojima et al. 1999; Tschop, Smiley, & Heiman 2000). The active form is a 28-amino-acid peptide hormone, which is generated by proteolytic cleavage of preproghrelin and proghrelin. To become the acylated form, a posttranslational acylation take place. Both acylated and de-acylated ghrelin seem to be able to cross the blood–brain barrier, but only the acylated form is able to activate central growth-hormone secretagogue receptors (GHS-R1A) in the hypothalamus (Banks et al. 2002). Ghrelin increases body weight and food intake by stimulating the production of neuropeptide Y and agouti-related protein in the nucleus arcuatus and leads to elevated concentrations of plasma glucose and the stimulation of lipogenesis (Hagemann et al. 2003).

Furthermore, ghrelin signals have also been identified in brain regions outside the hypothalamus, such as mesolimbic dopaminergic pathways (Andrews 2011). In the ventral tegmental area (VTA), ghrelin stimulates the firing rate of dopaminergic neurons through its effects on GHS-R1A receptors (Abizaid et al. 2006). Additionally, Weinberg, Nicholson, & Currie (2011) found that the infusion of dopaminergic neurotoxin 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) in the VTA suppressed ghrelin's ability to elicit food-reinforcing behavior. This finding underlines the hypothesis that ghrelin induces food-reinforcing behavior in the mesotelencephalic reward pathway and that this effect is dependent on its interaction with dopaminergic signaling. In the mesolimbic system, dopaminergic projections from the VTA extend into the nucleus accumbens, the ventral striatum (VS) and further into the prefrontal cortex. In this system, dopamine acts as a marker molecule for incentive sensitization and the dopaminergic neurons represent the locus of action for addictive compounds (Berridge & Robinson 1998). More recently, it has been postulated that a modulation of the ghrelin pathway also affects drug-seeking behavior (for an overview: Vengeliene 2013).

There is growing evidence that central ghrelin signaling plays a significant role in the regulation of alcohol consumption by modulating the activity of dopaminergic neurons (for overview, see Koopmann, Schuster, & Kiefer 2016). Although there are some conflicting results (Schneider et al. 2007; Lyons et al. 2008; Jerlhag et al. 2014), the majority of preclinical studies evaluating ghrelin's effects on alcohol consumption suggest that the administration of ghrelin receptor antagonists decreases alcohol preference and consumption, while ghrelin administration enhances alcohol consumption (Kaur & Ryabinin 2010; Landgren et al. 2012; Suchankova et al. 2013; Gomez & Ryabinin 2014; Gomez et al. 2015; Stevenson et al. 2015; Stevenson et al. 2016). Investigations of the relationship between ghrelin plasma levels and alcohol craving in recent clinical studies furthermore showed a positive association between alcohol craving and ghrelin plasma concentrations (Addolorato et al. 2006; Hillemacher et al. 2007; Koopmann et al. 2012; Leggio et al. 2012; Akkişi Kumsar & Dilbaz 2015; Suchankova et al. 2016). There are only two interventional studies that focus on ghrelin's role in alcohol craving (Leggio et al. 2014; Koopmann et al. 2017). In a double-blind, placebo-controlled human laboratory study in nontreatment-seeking, heavy-drinking, alcohol-dependent individuals, Leggio et al. (2014) found that the application of exogenous ghrelin increased alcohol craving. In contrast, they did not find any effects of ghrelin administration related to the urge to drink juice or craving for food. Complementary to the results of Leggio et al. (2014), in a very recent randomized clinical study, we found that a modulation in the ghrelin system by inducing distention of the stomach via acute oral water intake (1000 ml still mineral water in 10 minutes) reduces craving in male patients suffering from alcohol dependence during early abstinence (Koopmann et al. 2017).

Altogether, the preclinical and clinical data thus far indicate that ghrelin modulates alcohol craving via mutual interactions with signaling of dopaminergic neurons.

Previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) alcohol cue-reactivity studies of our own group, investigating the same alcohol cue-reactivity paradigm, indicated robust activation mostly of occipital and frontal cortical regions in addition to limbic areas when investigating patient populations (e.g. Bach et al. 2015) and indicated cue-induced activation of the VS only when comparing alcohol dependent patients and healthy controls after small volume correction analyses (e.g. Vollstädt-Klein et al. 2012). Nonetheless, the VS seems to show the most stable activation during the confrontation with alcohol cues, best reliability, as well as the strongest association with relapse and craving in alcohol dependent patients when performing region of interest analyses (Schacht et al. 2011; Yalachkov, Kaiser, & Naumer 2012; Schacht, Anton, & Myrick 2013). Therefore, the current study specified the VS as region of interest. The aim of the present study was to investigate the relationship between ghrelin plasma concentrations, mesolimbic reactivity to alcohol cues and alcohol craving in recently detoxified patients suffering from alcohol dependence.

Materials and Methods

Study sample

Forty-one alcohol-dependent patients (11 female participants, 26.8 percent) were recruited during the first 21 days of controlled alcohol abstinence (mean = 10.1 days, standard deviation (SD) = 5.6 days after admission) from the inpatient unit of the Department of Addictive Behavior and Addiction Medicine at the Central Institute of Mental Health (Mannheim, Germany). All patients were screened prior to inclusion and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, in accordance to the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were included only if they (1) were between 18 and 65 years of age, (2) met the diagnostic criteria for alcohol dependence according to the Diagnostic Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) [as assessed using a standardized psychiatric interview (SCID-I)], (3) did not meet criteria for any other Axis-I disorders except from nicotine dependence in the last 12 months [as assessed using a standardized psychiatric interview (SCID-I)], (4) did not take any psychoactive substances, anti-craving or anticonvulsive medication (defined as a time interval of at least five half-lives since the last time of medication administration), (5) did not have any comorbid severe internal or neurological conditions, (6) did not have a positive drug-screening and (7) did not meet any contraindications for receiving a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-scan (e.g. tattoos, metal implants, pregnancy and pacemakers). The parent multicenter study (http://clinicaltrials.gov registration number: NCT010503931) was conducted in three study centers in Germany (University Hospital of Berlin, University Hospital of Bonn and Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim). Hormonal analyses of the appetite regulating peptide ghrelin presented in this paper were only performed for the subsample recruited in Mannheim. The current study and all experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the University of Heidelberg (registration number: 2006-153N-MA).

Demographical data, clinical variables and plasma ghrelin levels

To ensure that the participants were not under the influence of alcohol or other drugs on the same day as the scan, a breath alcohol test and a urine screening test for commonly abused drugs as well as for benzodiazepines, which were administered during detoxification, were completed prior to the scan. In addition to providing demographic data, all participants were asked to complete a number of questionnaires, including the Alcohol Dependence Scale (ADS; Kivlahan, Sher, & Donovan 1989) and the Alcohol Urge Questionnaire (AUQ; Bohn, Krahn, & Staehler 1995) as well as the Beck Depression Inventory (Hautzinger et al. 2009) and the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (Spielberger, 1970). The latter two questionnaires were administered in order to gather more precise information about the psychopathological symptoms of the participants. In addition, a short semi-structured interview was performed with every participant in order to assess their alcohol consumption during 90 days prior to the experiment (Form 90; Sobell et al. 1996). Furthermore, blood samples were drawn from all participants to measure plasma levels of acylated and total ghrelin. All samples were obtained at 8:00 AM after overnight fasting (8 hours) by venepuncture and anti-coagulated with sodium EDTA (1 mg/ml whole blood) and then immediately cooled on ice. Plasma was separated in a centrifuge at 4000 g, and aliquots were immediately frozen and stored at −80°C until the time of analysis (max. 6 months). Hormonal analyses were performed in the Neurobiology Lab at the Department of Psychiatry at the University Medical Center, Hamburg (KW).

The plasma concentrations of active acylated ghrelin were measured using a radioimmunoassay kit (DRG Instruments, Marburg, Germany) with the detection limit set to 7.8 pg/ml. The inter-assay coefficient of variation was less than 9.6 percent; the intra-assay coefficient was less than 9.5 percent.

We measured plasma concentrations of total ghrelin using a radioimmunoassay kit (DRG-Instruments, Marburg, Germany) with the detection limit set to 93 pg/ml. The inter-assay coefficient of variation was less than 9.8 percent; the intra-assay coefficient was less than 10 percent.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging alcohol cue-reactivity task

Directly after the blood was drawn, all participants underwent a visual alcohol cue-reactivity task that was implemented and validated in previous studies by our research group (e.g. Vollstadt-Klein et al. 2012). During the fMRI scanning session, 12 blocks of alcohol-related images (with sequences of five pictures in one block) and 9 blocks neutral images (with sequences of five pictures in one block) were presented in a pseudo-randomized order using MRI compatible goggles (MRI Audio/Video Systems, Resonance Technology Inc., Los Angeles, CA, USA). Each picture was presented for 4 seconds. In between successive blocks, participants were asked to report their current craving for alcohol according to a visual analogue scale from 0 (‘no craving at all’) to 100 (‘very intense craving’). Participants provided 12 craving ratings after alcohol picture blocks and 9 ratings after neutral picture blocks. The mean alcohol craving across all 12 alcohol blocks and the mean craving across the 9 neutral blocks were computed and implemented in all further analyses. Image presentation and the recording of craving data were controlled using the Presentation® software (Version 16.0, Neurobehavioral Systems Inc., Albany, CA, USA). The entire cue-reactivity task consisted of 21 picture presentation blocks and took approximately 12 minutes in total.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging acquisition, pre-processing and statistical analyses

The functional MRI was performed using a Siemens MAGNETOM 3 Tesla whole-body-tomograph (MAGNETOM Trio, TIM technology, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). For each participant, a total of 305 T2*-weighted echo-planar images were acquired during the alcohol cue-reactivity task with standardized imaging parameters (TR = 2.41 s, TE = 25 ms, flip angle = 80°, 42 slices, slice thickness = 2 mm, 1-mm gap, voxel dimensions 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, FOV = 192 × 192 mm2, 64 × 64 in-plane resolution).

In order to minimize the risk of artifacts due to magnetic saturation effects, the first five MRI images were eliminated from all subsequent analyses. Remaining MRI data was pre-processed according to standard procedures implemented in the statistical parametric mapping software for Matlab (SPM, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK) version 5 [i.e. spatial realignment, movement correction, normalization to a standard Montreal Neurological Institute (Quebec, Canada) echo-planar images template and smoothing using an isotropic Gaussian kernel for group analysis (8 mm full width at half maximum)]. Additional control of excessive movement during the scanning session was established by inspection of the translation (x, y and z axis) and rotation (roll, yaw and pitch) estimates in SPM8. Datasets were excluded if the translation was greater than 2 mm or if the rotation (in any direction) was greater than two degrees. Two participants had to be excluded from further analyses due to excessive movement during the scanning session. First level statistics were computed for each participant, modeling the different experimental conditions (alcohol pictures, neutral pictures and rating phase) in a generalized linear model. Resulting contrast images (‘alcohol–neutral’) were imputed in second-level analyses using SPM8. Whole brain functional alcohol cue-induced brain responses across all participants were investigated using a one-sample t-test. In addition, multiple regression analyses including leverage statistics (Cook's d) were carried out in order to test for associations between plasma levels of acylated as well as total ghrelin and alcohol cue-induced brain response. Previous research has noted associations between body mass index (BMI) and the ghrelin-O-acyltransferase, which is responsible for the posttranslational acylation of ghrelin to its biologically active form in humans (Goebel-Stengel et al. 2013). For this reason, individual BMI was included as a covariate in the multiple regression analyses. Moreover, past work has suggested that functional cue-induced brain response is influenced by the time that has elapsed since the last alcohol consumption (Fryer et al. 2013). Therefore, abstinence prior to MRI scanning was also included as a covariate in the analyses. In order to control for multiple comparisons, a combined voxel wise and cluster extent threshold, corresponding to a family wise error (FWE) rate of PFWE < 0.05, was determined using AlphaSim as implemented in the NeuroElf toolbox (www.neuroelf.net) for Matlab. For a pre-set voxel wise threshold of P < 0.001, the AlphaSim procedure determined a cluster extent threshold of 39 voxels (10.000 Monte Carlo simulations, estimated smoothness based on the residual images was x = 10.74 mm, y = 10.76 mm and z = 9.72 mm). All thresholds for the fMRI analyses were set accordingly. In addition to whole brain analyses, associations between alcohol cue-induced brain activation in the VS, behavioral measures and acylated ghrelin concentrations were investigated by extracting mean functional cue-induced activation from the VS, defined using a standardized anatomical mask from the Nielsen and Hansen database (Hansen et al., 2002), with a custom SPM toolbox that is described in detail by Reinhard and colleagues (Reinhard et al. 2015). The mean functional activation was computed across all voxels in the predefined VS mask.

Statistical analyses of demographic and clinical data as well as plasma peptide levels

We used the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24.0 for Windows to perform our data analyses. Descriptive results are reported as means and SDs. The significance level for all analyses was set to α = 0.05. As mentioned earlier, previous research was able to show that the activity of the ghrelin-O-acyltransferase, which is responsible for the posttranslational acylation of ghrelin to its biologically active form in humans (Goebel-Stengel et al. 2013), is influenced by BMI. Therefore, partial correlation analyses controlling for BMI were implemented to test for associations between clinical variables and plasma peptide levels and for associations between mean functional alcohol cue-induced activation that was extracted from the VS region of interest and clinical data (e.g. subjective craving). In addition, associations between ghrelin concentration, subjective craving and mean functional brain response in the VS were investigated by applying linear regression analyses. Linearity of associations between independent and dependent variables, homoscedasticity, independence of errors and non-deviation from normal distribution of errors were confirmed by visual inspection. Multicollinearity was assessed by examining tolerance and the Variance Inflation Factor. A mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro for Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 2.16.3, http://www.processmacro.org), in order to investigate whether cue-induced brain response mediates the effect of ghrelin on subjective craving for alcohol (Hayes 2013). The indirect effect of ghrelin on subjective craving was tested using a bootstrap estimation approach with 5000 samples and a confidence interval of 95 percent (Hayes 2013).

Hormonal analyses

The mean plasma concentration for acylated ghrelin was 65.52 pg/ml (SD = 69.7) and 959.15 pg/ml (SD = 406.4 pg/ml) for total ghrelin.

Associations between ghrelin concentration and clinical data

Mean subjective alcohol cue-induced craving during the scanning session correlated positively with acylated ghrelin plasma concentrations (r = 0.316, P = 0.027). Furthermore, we found a significant positive association between plasma concentrations of acylated ghrelin and the sum score of the AUQ (r = 0.406, P = 0.040). In contrast, we found no association between total ghrelin plasma levels and mean subjective alcohol craving (r = −0.111, P = 0.254) as well as the AUQ (r = 0.183, P = 0.370).

Functional magnetic resonance imaging alcohol cue-induced brain activation

Alcohol stimuli relative to neutral stimuli (contrast: ‘alcohol–neutral’) induced brain activation in the superior and inferior occipital gyri, the fusiform gyrus, the calcarine, the lingual gyrus, the insula, the superior, middle and inferior orbitofrontal cortex and the cerebellum (Table 2a and Figure 1). On the other hand, contrasting neutral pictures with alcohol-related pictures (contrast: ‘neutral–alcohol’) revealed significant activation in the lingual gyrus, the fusiform gyrus, the parahippocampal gyrus, the insula and the superior temporal gyrus (Table 2b).

Multiple regression analyses, controlling for BMI and abstinence prior to scanning, revealed a significant positive association between acylated ghrelin plasma levels and an alcohol-induced brain response (contrast: ‘alcohol–neutral’) in a network of brain clusters including the right VS, bilateral insulae, the anterior cingulum, parts of the frontal, temporal and occipital cortices and the cerebellum (Table 3 and Figure 2). In addition, mean alcohol cue-induced activation extracted from the VS, defined using a standardized anatomical mask, significantly correlated with plasma levels of acylated ghrelin (r = 0.616, P = 0.001) (Figure 3), thus corroborating the results of the whole brain analyses. There were no data points that surpassed a Cook's d value of >1 for the correlation of acylated ghrelin and VS activation (range of Cook's d: 0 to 0.244). In addition, our results demonstrate a significant association between VS activation and alcohol craving during the scanning session (r = 0.687, P = 0.001). There was no significant negative correlation between acylated ghrelin levels and the cue-induced blood oxygenation level dependent response. Furthermore, total ghrelin plasma levels were not significantly correlated with the cue-induced brain response.

Regression and mediation analyses

Linear regression analyses demonstrated that acylated ghrelin levels significantly predict craving for alcohol during the scanning session (F(1,38) = 4.187, P = 0.048, adjusted R2 = 0.076, b = 0.050, SE = 0.024) and mean cue reactivity in the VS (F(1,38) = 23.250, P = 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.363, b = 0.003, SE = 0.001). In addition, a significant effect of cue reactivity in the VS on alcohol craving was found (F(1,39) = 34.897, P = 0.001, b = 21.815, SE = 3.693), with an adjusted R2 of 0.459 (Figure 4). These results support the mediational hypothesis.

Further analyses indicated that acylated ghrelin (b = −0.027, SE = 0.023, P = 0.256) was no longer a significant predictor of craving after controlling for the mediator, cue reactivity in the VS (b = 25.039, SE = 4.731, P = 0.001), thus consistent with full mediation (Figure 4). The indirect effect of ghrelin on craving was tested using a bootstrap estimation approach with 5000 samples (Hayes 2013). Results demonstrate that the indirect effect was significant (b = 0.076, SE = 0.042, 95 percent CI = 0.006–0.020).

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to investigate the relationship between ghrelin plasma concentrations, mesolimbic reactivity to alcohol cues and alcohol craving in recently detoxified patients suffering from alcohol dependence. In the mediation analyses, we found that the significant positive association between acylated ghrelin plasma concentrations and alcohol craving is mediated by a cue-induced brain response in the VS.

In an environment deficient of nourishment, food-seeking behavior is influenced not only by homeostatic regulation but also by mechanisms of reinforcement that initiate learning processes that result in food-seeking behavior. Motivational pathways associated with caloric intake appear to be interrelated with elements of energy homeostasis; satiety factors seem to dampen the motivational response to food cues (Fernandes et al. 2013). However, if satiety factors contribute to the establishment of motivational mesolimbic pathways, they might also have a role in motivational behavior in general, including addictive behavior (Kiefer 2014; Koopmann et al. 2016).

The majority of previous clinical studies have found that the plasma concentration of ghrelin is associated with craving in alcohol-dependent patients. Akkişi Kumsar & Dilbaz (2015) showed a positive correlation between ghrelin concentrations and both, the total score and the score of the compulsive subscale of the Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS) as well as the score of the resistance subscale of the Penn Alcohol Craving Scale. In addition, our group demonstrated a significant positive correlation between plasma concentrations of acylated ghrelin and alcohol craving as measured with the OCDS at days 1 and 14 of detoxification in 61 alcohol-dependent male inpatient subjects (Koopmann et al. 2012). In a 12-week study by Leggio et al. (2012) on patients suffering from alcohol dependence, plasma ghrelin concentrations were positively correlated to craving as measured by the Penn Alcohol Craving Score after two weeks and with the Penn Alcohol Craving Scale score, a visual analogue scale and the sum score of the OCDS after 6 and 12 weeks. Furthermore, Addolorato et al. (2006) demonstrated a significant positive correlation between ghrelin plasma concentrations and alcohol craving in a very early investigation of alcohol-dependent individuals.

While results of previous research concerning the association between ghrelin plasma concentrations and alcohol craving in recently detoxified, alcohol-dependent patients are quite homogenous, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of this phenomenon are not fully understood. In a very recent preclinical study, Kalafateli et al. (2018) was able to show that a systemic intraperitoneal administration of rimonabant, a cannabinoid receptor antagonist, attenuated ghrelin's intracerebroventricular ability to cause locomotor stimulation and nucleus accumbens dopamine release in mice. In contrast, ghrelin-induced chow intake was not altered by rimonabant administration. Additionally, the authors found that bilateral VTA administration of rimonabant blocks the ability of intra-VTA administered ghrelin to increase locomotor activity but does not affect food intake in mice. Altogether, these data suggest a clear dissociation between the regulation of food intake and the activation of the mesolimbic dopamine system. Meanwhile, in another recent clinical study, Malik et al. (2008) found that the intravenous administration of de-acylated ghrelin in healthy volunteers during fMRI increased the neural response to food pictures in various regions of the brain including the amygdala, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior insula and striatum, moreover, implicating their role in encoding the incentive value of food cues. Additionally, the authors could show that the effects of ghrelin on the responses in the amygdala and OFC were correlated with self-rated hunger. These results suggest that metabolic signals such as ghrelin may favor food consumption by enhancing the hedonic and incentive responses to food-related cues. In relation to the finding that food consumption is driven not only by homeostatic regulation mechanisms but also by the hedonic value of food, Farokhnia et al. (2017) found that an intravenous administration of acylated ghrelin increased alcohol-related signaling in the amygdala and modulated food-related signaling in the medial orbitofrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens in nontreatment-seeking, alcohol-dependent, heavy-drinking individuals during fMRI. In an additional experiment on alcohol self-administration, the authors furthermore found that an i.v. administration of acylated ghrelin significantly increased the number of self-administered alcohol infusions compared to placebo and that the participants initiated alcohol self-administration significantly more quickly when they received ghrelin compared to placebo. These results indicate that the exogenous administration of ghrelin seems to affect neuronal activation in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system in alcohol-dependent patients, while it is not entirely clear exactly how endogenous ghrelin affects neuronal activation in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system and how such changes in mesolimbic neuronal activation affect patients' subjective alcohol craving. Ralevski et al. (2017) found that fasting plasma levels of acylated and total ghrelin were significant predictors of alcohol-related stimulant and sedative effects in a sample of healthy social drinkers. They were able to show that higher fasting ghrelin levels are associated with longer lasting and more intense subjective effects. Additionally, the same group was able to show that healthy social drinkers with higher levels of fasting ghrelin are more sensitive to reward but less sensitive to punishment conditions. They also found that higher ghrelin levels were furthermore related to certain aspects of impulsivity such as decreased self-control and increased likelihood of acting without thinking (Ralevski et al. 2018). In demonstrating that the association between ghrelin plasma concentrations and alcohol craving is mediated by mesolimbic cue reactivity, our results suggest that endogenous ghrelin influences craving via changes in mesolimbic brain activation. This modulation is potentially driven by ghrelin's effects on GHS-R1A receptors in the VTA, where ghrelin stimulates the firing rate of dopaminergic neurons. Mesolimbic cue reactivity might then result in increased craving. Banks et al. (2002) found that only the active acylated form of ghrelin is able to activate central GHS-R1A. As previously mentioned, these GHS-R1A receptors are responsible for ghrelins' actions in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, which might explain why it was not possible to establish any association between plasma concentrations of total ghrelin and mesolimbic cue reactivity.

Notable strengths of the current study include the evaluation of a well-characterized clinical sample that took no psychoactive substances, anti-craving or anticonvulsive medication. In addition, the whole-brain analyses of imaging data allowed for the detection of significant brain activation without constraints to predefined region of interests. Furthermore, the consideration of covariates that were suggested in previous publications to affect ghrelin plasma concentrations and cue-induced brain activation in fMRI (e.g. BMI and abstinence prior to scanning) facilitates the assessment of correlated variables. Additionally, the separate analyses for total and acylated ghrelin contribute to an understanding of the distinguishable effects of the ghrelin system on the mesolimbic dopaminergic system. A limitation of the current study is the relatively small sample size, especially for female subjects. Future studies including a greater number of female participants are needed in order to address the question of how the ghrelin system affects craving in this particular cohort.

Altogether, our results might help clarifying the interactions between food consumption, subjective craving and cue-induced activity of the mesolimbic dopaminergic system in addictive disorders, in addition underlining the necessity for monitoring food consumption when testing mesolimbic cue reactivity in addictive disorders. Our findings support the idea that modulating the ghrelin system seems to represent a valid treatment target for reducing cue reactivity in addictive disorders. Reducing plasma levels of acylated ghrelin through behavioral interventions or antagonizing ghrelin at the GHS-R1A receptor may lead to new and innovative approaches to the treatment of craving in alcohol dependence.
