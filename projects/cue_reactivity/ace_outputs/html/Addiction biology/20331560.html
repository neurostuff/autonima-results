ABSTRACT

Smoking-related cues elicit craving and mesocorticolimbic brain activation in smokers. Severity of nicotine dependence seems to moderate cue reactivity, but the direction and mechanisms of its influence remains unclear. Although tobacco control policies demand a ban on tobacco advertising, cue reactivity studies in smokers so far have not employed tobacco advertisement as experimental stimuli. We investigated whether tobacco advertisement elicits cue reactivity at a behavioral (subjective craving) and a neural level (using functional magnetic resonance imaging) in 22 smokers and 21 never-smokers. Moreover, we studied the influence of severity of dependence on cue reactivity. In smokers, tobacco advertisement elicited substantially more craving than control advertisement whereas never-smokers reported no cue induced craving. Surprisingly, neuronal cue reactivity did not differ between smokers and never-smokers. Moderately dependent smokers' craving increased over the course of the experiment, whereas highly dependent smokers' craving was unaffected. Moderately dependent smokers' brain activity elicited by tobacco advertisement was higher in the amygdala, hippocampus, putamen and thalamus compared with highly dependent smokers. Furthermore, limbic brain activation predicted picture recognition rates after the scanning session, even in never-smokers. Our findings show that tobacco advertisement elicits cigarette craving and neuronal cue reactivity primarily in moderately dependent smokers, indicating that they might be particularly responsive towards external smoking-related cues. On the other hand, neuronal cue reactivity and cigarette craving in highly dependent smokers is more likely triggered by internal cues such as withdrawal symptoms. Tobacco advertisement seems to likewise appeal to smokers and non-smokers, clarifying the potential danger especially for young non-smokers.

INTRODUCTION

Cue reactivity denotes reactions in response to drug associated cues (like drug utensils, environments or advertisements) which appear on subjective, physiological, and behavioural levels (e.g. drug craving, changes in heart rate, neural activations, relapse, amount of consumption, etc. (Drummond 2001)). These cue-induced reactions are highly associated with compulsive uncontrolled drug intake and are major triggers for relapse (Weiss et al. 2001). According to conditioning theories of drug addiction, cue reactivity consists of conditioned reactions whereupon primarily neutral stimuli become associated with drug consumption and, after development of a drug addiction, these cues are able to evoke drug-like effects on their own (Drummond 2001). According to the incentive-sensitization theory, drug stimuli acquire high incentive salience during the development of addiction due to sensitization of the mesolimbic dopamine system (Robinson & Berridge 2001). Other theories of drug addiction focus on the role of habit rather than incentive processes. The incentive-habit model suggests that, in highly dependent smokers, weaker incentive effects can be found and that they smoke largely habitually, requiring little conscious control compared with light smokers (Di Chiara 2000; Mogg, Field & Bradley 2005). In highly dependent smokers, habitual smoking behavior is automatically initiated by drug cues in the absence of incentive motivational processes (Everitt, Dickinson & Robbins 2001). Therefore, the strength of incentive motivational responding may diminish as habit becomes more pronounced during the development of nicotine addiction.

Using neuroimaging techniques, neural networks have been identified that are activated by drug-related cues in individuals addicted to drugs like cocaine, alcohol or nicotine (Childress et al. 1999; Garavan et al. 2000; Braus et al. 2001; George et al. 2001; Schneider et al. 2001; Wrase et al. 2002; Grüsser et al. 2004; McBride et al. 2006; Brody et al. 2007; Franklin et al. 2007). Brain regions most commonly implicated in cue reactivity include structures tightly associated with mesolimbic dopaminergic pathways as well as attention-related areas, namely the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLFPC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), insula, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus as well as occipital areas. Smokers compared with never-smokers showed increased cue-induced brain activity in mesolimbic structures (amygdala, hippocampus, ventral tegmental area (VTA), thalamus, prefrontal cortex (PFC) as well as in the cingulate gyrus, the fusiform gyrus, parietal and prefrontal areas (Brody et al. 2002; Due et al. 2002). In a group of smokers ranging from non-dependent to heavy-dependent, we identified independent brain networks where cue reactivity was associated with severity of nicotine dependence (regions involved in motor preparation, imagery and visuospatial attention) and subjective craving (mesocorticolimbic areas), respectively (Smolka et al. 2006). A recent study of McClernon and colleagues suggests that factors like gender, withdrawal and severity of nicotine dependence modulate smoking related neuronal cue reactivity (McClernon, Kozink & Rose 2008). Severely dependent smokers showed more cue-induced brain activation in the basal ganglia, the cingulate, orbitofrontal and inferior occipital gyrus, but less activation in parietal, temporal, fusiform and parahippocampal areas.

Tobacco advertisements are smoking-related cues which appear in everyday life. In the last years, prohibition of tobacco advertisement has been an important topic in policy. A directive by the European Union (EU) from 2003 prohibits tobacco advertisement in all public media except on billboards and in cinemas (EU directive #2003/33/EG, which came into effect in 2005). Tobacco advertisement is regarded as dangerous because of its known influence on smoking initiation (DiFranza et al. 2006). Adolescents are more influenced by tobacco advertisements compared with adults and, therefore, are more likely to take the bait to smoke (Pierce et al. 1991; Lovato et al. 2003). The neural substrates underlying the processing of cigarette advertisement have not been examined. To our knowledge, there is only one study using visual alcohol advertisement stimuli to examine cue reactivity: Tapert et al. (2003) found increased activation in adolescents with alcohol use disorder in limbic, visual and anterior areas in response to alcohol advertisement (Tapert et al. 2003). In the present study, we used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to assess the neural processing of tobacco advertisements in nicotine-dependent smokers and never-smokers. We supposed tobacco advertisement stimuli to be especially efficacious in eliciting craving and cue-induced brain activation, because they are designed by commercial artists. As tobacco advertisement mainly uses pictures of cigarettes or people who are smoking, we expected it to activate the same brain regions as smoking related cues used in tobacco cue reactivity studies. In addition, we expected severity of nicotine dependence to modulate subjective craving and neuronal cue reactivity in smokers. The direction of its influence and the involved brain regions are not consistent between studies. Therefore, we used an explorative correlational approach to examine the association between severity of dependence and cue reactivity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

Forty-eight healthy male participants (24 non-abstinent smokers and 24 never-smokers) were initially recruited. All participants provided written informed consent according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Heidelberg. Participants were contacted either personally by one of the authors or responded to a flyer on a bulletin board in a university building. Data from five participants were excluded from statistical analysis because of excessive movement during the fMRI scan. The final sample consisted of 22 non-abstinent smokers (age 31 ± 7 years) and 21 never-smokers (age 29 ± 5 years), all physically healthy, male and right-handed. To exclude axis 1 psychiatric disorders the Munich-Composite International Diagnostic Interview (Wittchen et al. 1998) was conducted. Participants did not take any medication on a regular basis or acute medication in the last 7 days. They had no current or past substance abuse disorders (besides nicotine dependence in the smoker group) nor any other psychiatric disorders and no current illicit drug consumption (verified by drug screening tests). Handedness was assessed via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield 1971). Only participants with a laterality quotient greater than 50 and a sufficient visual acuity [binocular (corrected) ≥ 0.8] were included.

The inclusion criterion for never-smokers was a cigarette consumption of at most 20 cigarettes a lifetime. Inclusion criteria for smokers were a minimum of 15 cigarettes per day within the last 4 weeks and nicotine dependence according to DSM IV. Severity of smokers' nicotine dependence was measured with the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND (Heatherton et al. 1991)). Smokers were nicotine dependent with FTND scores of 6.3 ± 1.5 (minimum 2, maximum 9). Their smoking onset was at the age of 16.8 ± 2.1. They had smoked for 14.6 ± 7.3 years. The mean number of cigarettes per day was 24.6 ± 7.2. None of the smokers was explicitly treatment seeking. Smokers were not abstinent and smoked just prior to the scanning session.

Task

Forty-five smoking-related and 45 control stimuli were presented in a blocked design manner with nine blocks per category. One block consisted of five stimuli, each presented for 6.6 seconds, resulting in a total duration of 33 seconds per block. After each block participants were asked to indicate their craving (‘How much do you crave for a cigarette?’) on a visual analogue scale ranging between 0 (‘no craving at all’) and 100 (‘extreme craving’). Unlike physiological measures these craving ratings describe participants' craving only on a subjective basis. Participants had to assess their craving within a period of 9.9 seconds. Thereafter, a black fixation cross was presented on a white background for a minimum of 3.3 seconds so that the total duration of craving rating and fixation cross was 13.2 seconds altogether. Task duration was 15 minutes.

Tobacco advertisement taken from print media served as smoking-related visual cues. In 2003, the European Union finalised a directive to outlaw tobacco advertisement in all public media with the exception of billboard advertisement and cinema advertisement. Germany transposed this directive into national law at the end of 2006, just immediately after the data acquisition phase for the present study. Therefore, participants were still familiar with tobacco advertisement in print media. Control cues were taken from printed advertisements from the areas household, personal hygiene and clothing. Stimuli contained little text and few people. Control advertisings were matched to tobacco advertisings regarding complexity, colours and content. Examples of the stimuli presented during the fMRI scan are provided in Fig. 1. The order of the categories tobacco and control was pseudo-randomized whereas the order of the pictures was randomized for each subject. After the scanning session participants performed an unannounced recognition task, to test whether they paid sufficient attention to the stimuli during the fMRI session. Ten previously shown and ten new pictures (also drawn from the advertisement categories tobacco and control) were presented and had to be assigned as ‘previously seen’ or ‘not seen before’.

fMRI acquisition

Scanning was performed with a 1.5 T whole-body tomograph (Magnetom VISION; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a standard quadrature head coil. For functional imaging, 30 transversal slices (thickness of 4 mm, gap of 1 mm) were acquired every 3300 ms using a standard EPI sequence [TR (repetition time) 0.6 ms, TE (echo time) 60 ms, flip angle 90°] with an in-plane resolution of 64 × 64 pixels (FOV 220 mm) resulting in a voxel size of 3.44 × 3.44 × 5 mm3. For anatomical reference, a 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) image data set was acquired (TR = 11.4 ms, TE = 4.4 ms, FOV = 256 mm, 162 slices, 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 voxel size, flip angle = 12°).

Images were presented via goggles using MRI Audio/Video Systems (Resonance Technology Inc., Los Angeles, USA). Task presentation and recording of the behavioural responses was performed using Presentation® software (Version 9.9, Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Albany, CA, USA).

fMRI preprocessing

Pre-processing as well as statistical analyses of brain imaging data were performed using SPM5 (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK). Each session comprised 264 scans. The first five scans were excluded from the analyses to avoid artifacts due to magnetic saturation effects. The remaining 259 scans were spatially realigned to correct for head motion over the course of the session and then normalized to an MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute, Quebec, Canada) EPI template, resampled with 3 × 3 × 3 mm3 voxels. Subsequent smoothing was done using an isotropic Gaussian kernel for group analysis (12 mm FWHM).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the pre-processed fMRI data on first (individual) level was performed by modelling the different conditions (tobacco advertisement blocks, control advertisement blocks and rating periods; boxcar functions convoluted with the hemodynamic response function) as explanatory variables within the context of the general linear model (GLM) on a voxel-by-voxel basis with SPM5. To identify brain activation differences in each group (one sample t-test), group differences (two sample t-test) and associations with severity of nicotine dependence (correlation), individual contrast images (‘tobacco—control advertisement’) of the participants were included in second level analyses. Significance level was set to P < 0.05 (FDR corrected) on a cluster level of ≥10 adjacent voxels. For demonstration purposes, we used stricter thresholds down to P < 0.01 (FDR corrected) when result tables were too large and complex due to wide area activation networks. To illustrate the association between cue reactivity and severity of dependence, we extracted mean parameter estimates from the first level statistics [contrast (‘tobacco—control advertisement’)] with bilateral region of interest (ROI) masks for the amygdala, the putamen, the hippocampus and the parahippocampus obtained from the Nielsen and Hansen database (Nielsen & Hansen 2002) which we used for creating scatter plots. To assess the association between cue-reactivity and performance in the subsequent recognition task (i.e. percentage of correct answers), we used bivariate correlation analyses, and additionally extracted mean parameter estimates for the contrasts ‘tobacco advertisement—rest’ and ‘control advertisement—rest’. Due to technical problems (response recording) in three cases, recognition task data from only 40 participants were available (20 smokers, 20 never-smokers).

Behavioural data (i.e. subjective craving data and FTND scores) were analyzed using Statistical Package of the Social Sciences (SPSS, Version 15.0.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). To assess the association between subjective craving during the scanner session, smoking status (never-smoker, smoker) and category (tobacco or control advertisement), a repeated measures analysis of variance (anova) was conducted with craving as dependent variable, smoking status as between-subject factor and category and time (nine block presentations per category) as within-subject factors. Estimated marginal means from this anova were used to describe craving in smokers and never-smokers. Associated tests for simple main effects were conducted to assess cue-induced craving (i.e. craving after tobacco versus control advertisement). In smokers, we additionally conducted a repeated measures anova with FTND as covariate to assess the association between FTND, craving (dependent variable) and the factors category and time.

For subgroup analyses, we split the group of smokers into a group of moderately dependent smokers (FTND ≤ 6, n = 12) and a group of heavily dependent smokers (FTND ≥ 7, n = 10). Age of smoking onset did not differ between moderate and heavy smokers. Heavily dependent smokers, however, were older than moderate smokers (heavy: 35.1 ± 7.0 years, moderate: 28.3 ± 4.3 years, P = 0.020) and never-smokers (28.8 ± 5.1 years, P = 0.017), probably as a consequence of increasing severity of dependence with increasing age. We therefore additionally calculated partial correlations between brain activation and FTND scores controlling for age, which yielded similar results as the bivariate correlations. Moreover, partial correlation analyses controlling for age were calculated to assess the association between brain activation and recognition task performance, which again led to similar results as the bivariate correlations. Therefore, we only report bivariate correlations throughout the paper.

RESULTS

Behavioural data

Smokers reported substantially more subjective craving after presentation of tobacco advertisement (estimated mean ± SE: 41.3 ± 3.1) than after control advertisement (estimated mean ± SE: 27.8 ± 2.3), P = 0.0000003. In contrast, never-smokers reported almost no craving at all (estimated mean ± SE: 1.1 ± 2.9 in the tobacco and 0.2 ± 2.2 in the control condition) and there was no difference between both conditions (P = 0.660). This ‘cue-induced craving’[traditionally defined as the difference between craving after tobacco and after neutral blocks (Franken 2003)] was not associated with severity of nicotine dependence (interaction advertisement category × FTND n.s., P = 0.646). However, craving increased during the course of the experiment (main effect of time, P = 0.0004, linear increase, P = 0.15) due to the continuous presentation of tobacco cues. This increase of craving during the course of the scanning session was modulated by severity of dependence (interaction time × FTND, P = 0.018). To illustrate how this interaction emerged, we compared moderately dependent with highly dependent smokers. In moderately dependent smokers, we found a significant increase in subjective craving during presentation of tobacco and control advertisement over the course of the experiment (main effect of time, P = 0.0004, linear increase, P = 0.024) whereas in highly dependent smokers no change of craving over time (P = 0.462) could be found (see Fig. 2). It can be assumed that participants viewed stimuli attentively as indicated by the good performance in the recognition task (87.0 ± 9.5 percent correct, range 65–100). There was no performance difference in the post-scan recognition task between smokers (88.8 ± 10.4 percent correct, range 65–100) and never-smokers (85.23 ± 8.4 percent correct, range 70–95), P = 0.247. Taken together, the behavioural data show that the fMRI paradigm works well and is suitable to evoke craving in smokers.

fMRI data

Smokers as well as never-smokers showed increased activation in response to tobacco compared with control advertisement in various brain structures throughout all brain lobes. Smokers additionally activated the middle cingulate gyrus bilaterally. Activation patterns of smokers and never-smokers are illustrated in Fig. 3 (also see Supporting Information Tables S1 and S2). Surprisingly, a direct comparison between smokers' and never-smokers' brain activation elicited by tobacco versus control advertisement revealed no significant differences (even at a more liberal threshold of P < 0.005 uncorrected). Concerning the association between severity of nicotine dependence and neuronal cue reactivity in the group of smokers our analysis revealed solely negative correlations, i.e. less nicotine-dependent smokers revealed higher cue-elicited activations throughout all brain lobes. Interestingly, the negative correlation of cue-elicited brain activation and severity of dependence was most pronounced in limbic areas (amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampus, putamen) and the thalamus (Fig. 4, Supporting Information Table S3). Furthermore, in all participants, the recognition rates for tobacco advertisements in the unannounced memory test conducted immediately after the MR scan were positively associated with the activation elicited by these stimuli in the amygdala (r = 0.298; P = 0.030), hippocampus (r = 0.360; P = 0.011) and parahippocampus (r = 0.378; P = 0.008).

DISCUSSION

Our study clearly shows that tobacco advertisement elicits subjective craving in smokers. The main finding is that cue reactivity decreases with increasing severity of nicotine dependence. Moderately dependent smokers were particularly responsive to tobacco advertisement, indicated by their increasing craving during the course of the cue reactivity experiment. On the other hand, heavily dependent smokers' craving was stable over time. In line with this, neuronal cue reactivity also decreased with increasing severity of nicotine dependence. In moderately dependent smokers cue reactivity seems to be elicited by external cues like tobacco advertisement. However, in heavily dependent smokers, who showed only modest neuronal reactivity to tobacco advertisement, cue reactivity could primarily be triggered by internal rather than external cues (i.e. physical withdrawal symptoms caused by nicotine deprivation).

Tobacco advertisement-induced brain activation was found in the same networks engaged in the processing of other smoking-related stimuli (Smolka et al. 2006; Brody et al. 2007; Franklin et al. 2007), namely in reward-related brain areas (frontal cortex), attention related regions (occipital cortex) and regions responsible for object and face recognition (temporal gyrus). The negative association between cue reactivity and severity of dependence was particularly pronounced in limbic and reward-related areas (amygdala, putamen, thalamus, hippocampus, parahippocampus). Similar brain regions are involved in the processing of smoking stimuli in moderate smokers (Franklin et al. 2007). Regions with an increased brain activation in lighter smokers included areas associated with memory (parahippocampal gyrus, hippocampus) and attention (parietal areas) which is partly in line with a study of McClernon and colleagues who also found negative associations between fMRI cue reactivity and severity of nicotine dependence in the parahippocampal gyrus, superior and inferior parietal regions (McClernon et al. 2008). Contrary to our results, they detected positive correlations between severity of dependence and cue-induced brain activation in the basal ganglia, the anterior cingulate cortex as well as orbitofrontal and inferior occipital regions. But the McClernon et al. study is not directly comparable to our study because of different design (event-related), participants (more than half of them desired to quit smoking) and stimuli (smoking-related objects and smoking people). In line with our results, a recent study of Artiges and colleagues (Artiges et al. 2009) found a cue-induced ‘deactivation’ in smokers, nearly in the same network where we found a ‘negative’ association between FTND and fMRI cue-reactivity, as well as a negative association between cue-induced insula activation and FTND. In an own study, we previously identified a brain network in which severity of nicotine dependence mediates neuronal cue reactivity in a small sample of 10 males ranging from occasional to highly dependent smokers (Smolka et al. 2006). Positive correlations between fMRI cue reactivity and FTND scores were found in structures involved in visuospatial attention as well as in motor regions which is contradictory to the results of our present study. However, the sample size of our previous study was very small and the range of FTND scores very wide, impeding a division into subgroups and a direct comparison of the studies. The negative association between cue reactivity and severity of nicotine dependence as well as the craving data are in line with an attentional bias study reporting longer gaze times for smoking cues with decreasing levels of nicotine dependence and with increasing levels of craving (Hogarth et al. 2003). Taken together, the findings suggest that less habitual smokers are more distracted by smoking-related cues than heavily dependent smokers who consume tobacco habitually and independently of external cues. These and our data on heavily dependent smokers support the incentive-habit model of addiction (Di Chiara 2000; Mogg et al. 2005), whereas our finding of neuronal and subjective cue reactivity in moderately dependent smokers is most adequately explained by the incentive-sensitization model (Robinson & Berridge 2001). According to the incentive-habit model, severely dependent smokers show weaker incentive effects due to more habitual drug use. Because habitual smoking is automatically initiated, highly dependent smokers might not need external cues like tobacco advertisement to initiate drug use.

In contrast, tobacco advertisement might be particularly potent in evoking cue reactivity in moderately dependent smokers. A neurobiological explanation for decreased neuronal cue reactivity in heavily dependent smokers could be a greater dysregulation of the dopaminergic mesocorticolimbic reward system in these individuals (Dagher et al. 2001; Fehr et al. 2008), which has been associated with the transition from controlled drug use to uncontrolled compulsive drug intake (Volkow & Fowler 2000).

Surprisingly, there was no difference in cue-induced brain activation between smokers and never-smokers. The variation of brain activations in never-smokers is high (see Fig. 4), indicating that tobacco advertisement induced a substantial response in some never-smokers which leads to a lack of difference between brain activations of never-smokers and moderate or heavy smokers. This unexpected result may be due to the fact that the presented tobacco stimuli were professionally designed advertisements which might have been equally attention drawing and pleasurable for a subgroup of non-smokers (displaying attractive persons, aesthetic compositions, beautiful nature sceneries, etc.). This potential overlap between brain structures involved in cue-reactivity and processing of pleasant stimuli (e.g. Putamen, Amygdala, Cingulate gyrus) might contribute to the lack of difference in brain activation between smokers and non-smokers and could explain the lack of association between craving and brain activation. Apparently, the tobacco-industry aims to create an image of their product that also appeals to (young) non-smokers, a strategy that seems to work: both smokers' and never-smokers' tobacco advertisement-induced brain activation in the amygdala, hippocampus and parahippocampus was positively associated with the recognition rate of these pictures in a subsequent memory test. This finding is in line with the amygdala's role in the enhancement of memory encoding during emotional processing (Kensinger & Schacter 2006). It is intriguing to speculate, that those never-smoking individuals who show high cue-induced brain reactivity might be particularly at risk to initiate smoking.

Furthermore, cue-induced brain activation does not necessarily reflect only subjective craving but could also be related to processes such as salience attribution and attention. We tested this assumption in our data by using correlational analyses and indeed found no significant association between brain activation and craving (data not reported). Consistently, other studies found correlations between subjective craving and cue-induced brain activation only in single regions like the sensorimotor cortex (Brody et al. 2002) or the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (McBride et al. 2006), and not in limbic or reward-related areas. It is therefore possible that the subjective craving measure differs from the objective physiological level. Moreover, the discrepancy between the behavioural and the neural data could be explained by an interfering reduction of brain activation in reward-related areas in smokers. This blunted reactivity could be due to a dysregulation of the dopaminergic mesocorticolimbic reward system in smokers (Fehr et al. 2008; Bühler et al. 2009).

Unfortunately, in the present study, we did not assess occasional smokers. However, we assume that cue reactivity may be a mechanism underlying drug use rather than drug addiction and, therefore, might be equally found in occasional smokers, which might explain, why tobacco advertisement is particularly dangerous for occasional (not yet dependent) smokers and why it increases the risk of smoking initiation (DiFranza et al. 2006). In alcohol dependence, cue reactivity was found to be a contributing factor in processes leading to relapse in abstinent dependent individuals (Grüsser et al. 2004). FMRI cue-reactivity in tobacco dependence could have much less influence on the maintenance of dependence or on relapse. For smokers, it was shown that increasing severity of dependence has a negative effect on abstinence (Piper, McCarthy & Baker 2006). Due to our results, we could even hypothesize a negative association between fMRI cue-reactivity and relapse. In contrast, the fMRI cue-reactivity that we found in never-smokers suggests that fMRI activation in response to tobacco advertisement might play a greater role in smoking initiation.

In Germany, tobacco advertisement is still allowed on billboards and in cinemas. According to the results of our study, prohibition of tobacco advertisement in all media could possibly reduce tobacco consumption, especially in moderately dependent smokers, because this group particularly shows strong cue reactivity in response to tobacco advertisement. Some other European countries (e.g. France, Italy, Portugal) already extended directive #2003/33/EG into a general prohibition of tobacco advertisement (http://ec.europa.eu/dgs/health_consumer/library/press/press258_de.pdf), but so far there are no data available about a possible reduction of smoking rates in these countries.

The decreased cue-induced brain activation in heavily dependent smokers indicates that the used cues might be more appropriate for studies on early or light dependence. Highly dependent smokers who seem to be less susceptible to external smoking cues, might probably be more responsive to other cues, e.g. stimuli associated with withdrawal.

To strengthen the hypotheses that cue reactivity is a central mechanism of substance use rather than drug addiction and to examine the neural basis of tobacco advertisement's impact on smoking initiation, longitudinal studies need to examine cue reactivity in non-dependent occasional and light dependent smokers. Future research should furthermore examine ethnicity because it has been shown to modulate cue-reactivity as well as smoking patterns (Okuyemi et al. 2006).

Despite these limitations, to our knowledge the current study is the first one providing evidence for cue reactivity elicited by tobacco advertisement. Moderately dependent smokers were particularly responsive to tobacco advertisement. In contrast, heavily dependent smokers seemed to be unaffected by tobacco related external cues. Our findings are partly in accordance with the incentive habit model of addiction, as highly dependent smokers show weaker incentive effects due to more habitual tobacco use. By contrast, the increased incentive salience for tobacco-related stimuli in moderately dependent smokers is consistent with the incentive-salience theory. Furthermore, the similar pattern of cue-induced brain activation in smokers and never-smokers in the presence of craving differences indicates that cue-induced brain activation can be used as a measure of cue-reactivity, but does not necessarily reflect cue-induced craving.
