Abstract

Alcohol Use Disorder has been associated with impairments of functional connectivity between neural networks underlying reward processing and cognitive control. Evidence for aberrant functional connectivity between the striatum, insula, and frontal cortex in alcohol users exists at rest, but not during cue-exposure. In this study, we investigated functional connectivity changes during a cue-reactivity task across different subgroups of alcohol consumers. Ninety-six participants (ranging from light social to heavy social drinkers and nonabstinent dependent to abstinent dependent drinkers) were examined. A functional magnetic resonance imaging cue-reactivity paradigm was administered, during which alcohol-related and neutral stimuli were presented. Applying psychophysiological interaction analyses, we found: (a) Abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with non-abstinent dependent drinkers showed a greater increase of functional connectivity of the ventral striatum and anterior insula with the anterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during the presentation of alcohol cues compared with neutral cues. (b) Subjective craving correlated positively with functional connectivity change between the posterior insula and the medial orbitofrontal cortex and negatively with functional connectivity change between the ventral striatum and the anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and lateral orbitofrontal cortex. (c) Compulsivity of alcohol use correlated positively with functional connectivity change between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the ventral striatum, anterior insula, and posterior insula. Results suggest increased cognitive control over cue-processing in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients, compensating high levels of cue-provoked craving and compulsive use.

1 INTRODUCTION

Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) represents a major health problem in today's society. AUD is characterized by a strong desire to consume alcohol (i.e. craving) and a failure to control alcohol intake. Furthermore, it is associated with aberrant functional organization in the brain. Brain regions which have received attention in AUD literature include the striatum, insula, orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC). It is widely accepted that the striatum plays a central role in reward processing, mediating alcohol's rewarding effects through an increase in dopamine, first in the ventral striatum and after continuing abuse in the dorsal striatum, pushing consumption to become a compulsive habit1. The insula has been associated with the translation of interoceptive signals into subjective experiences, e.g. craving. In AUD, these interoceptive representations have the capacity to “hijack” the cognitive resources necessary to exert inhibitory control over consumption urges2, 3. The OFC is a central region for motivation, drive, and salience attribution4, responsible for rendering alcohol cues highly salient compared with neutral cues in AUD individuals5. In contrast, the dlPFC is associated with cognitive control and restraining craving—functions, which are generally impaired in AUD4. The ACC interacts both with the OFC and dlPFC. Its ventral/rostral subdivision interacts with the OFC and is primarily involved in salience evaluation of emotional and motivational information and regulating emotional responses. The dorsal ACC interacts with the dlPFC and is responsible for inhibitory control and conflict monitoring4. The interaction and balance between these opposing cue-responsive and cognitive control centers is key in understanding the underlying neural mechanisms involved in AUD.

Comparing substance abusing individuals and healthy controls, resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) between striatum, OFC, and ACC4, 5 and between striatum, insula, and ACC6 was increased in substance abusers. By contrast, rsFC between dlPFC and both OFC and ACC was reduced in substance abusers4. The authors suggest that these neural abnormalities underlie an excessive influence of drug craving over behavioral guidance, while the ability to adapt behavior based on cognitive control and punishment evaluation (of negative consequences of drug use) may be impaired in substance abusers4, 5.

In abstaining AUD individuals, however, observations of rsFC suggest an increased involvement of cognitive control networks. Abstainers showed increased rsFC between striatum and dlPFC compared with relapsers, and between striatum, ACC, and dlPFC compared with controls7, progressively increasing with the duration of maintained abstinence8. The authors theorize that these changes in rsFC resemble an adaptive mechanism facilitating behavioral control necessary to maintain abstinence. In contrast, relapsers of AUD showed reduced rsFC between striatum, insula, ACC, and dlPFC when compared with abstainers, correlating with decreased inhibitory control9.

It is important to note that functional connectivity patterns can change according to contextual demands. A critical scenario for AUD individuals, for example, is the confrontation with alcohol-associated stimuli. The exposure to such stimuli can induce craving, i.e. a strong desire to consume alcohol10. Oftentimes, the elicited craving cannot be adequately inhibited and can consequently lead to actual consumption11. Therefore, understanding the effects of cue-exposure on functional connectivity may provide important insights for AUD treatment.

A common tool used to elucidate changes in functional connectivity in response to varying experimental contexts is psychophysiological interaction analysis (PPI)12. In the following, the terms “cue-induced functional connectivity” or “task-related coupling” will be used to refer to changes of functional connectivity due to a change of experimental condition. To date, PPI studies incorporating cue-reactivity tasks, in which substance-associated stimuli and neutral stimuli are presented in the MRI scanner, have been conducted with users of cannabis13, nicotine14-16, cocaine17, and alcohol18-20. In abstinent smokers, for example, cue-induced functional connectivity was increased between insula, ACC, and dlPFC compared with relapsers, suggesting an increased influence of cognitive control networks on interoception in abstainers when confronted with substance-related stimuli14. In AUD individuals, an association between greater AUD severity and weaker functional connectivity between striatum, anterior insula, and prefrontal cortex was observed during a response inhibition task, indicating reduced inhibitory control with increased AUD severity18. In a cue-reactivity task, abstinent AUD individuals showed increased cue-induced functional connectivity between midbrain and left OFC compared with both relapsers and healthy controls during the presentation of alcohol stimuli versus neutral stimuli20. Another study using the same paradigm further observed reduced task-related coupling between ventral striatum and dlPFC in AUD individuals compared with controls19. Overall, these PPI studies suggest that during substance cue-exposure, involvement of cognitive control networks is weaker in active substance abusers than in controls, progressing with substance use severity. Interestingly, these cognitive control networks seem to be upregulated in abstinent, treatment-receiving AUD individuals when compared with healthy controls. Unfortunately, a comparison between this group and non-abstinent, untreated AUD individuals was not included in the abovementioned studies. Concurrently analyzing a sample of untreated AUD individuals offers the possibility of detecting effects of treatment as well as persisting differences compared with healthy controls.

Our investigation aimed to (a) identify cue-induced changes of functional connectivity in AUD individuals, (b) compare these changes between subgroups of alcohol consumers varying in AUD severity and abstinence state (light drinkers, heavy drinkers, nonabstinent alcohol-dependent drinkers, and abstinent alcohol-dependent patients), and (c) determine whether cue-induced changes of functional connectivity are related to craving or compulsive use. To achieve these aims, we analyzed samples from prior investigations, including healthy controls and chronic alcohol users1, 21, as well as recently detoxified patients22, 23. As seed regions of interest for the PPI analyses, we chose the striatum and insula since both have consistently been activated by cue-reactivity tasks1, 20, 24-29 and their importance is supported by earlier PPI studies13, 14, 16, 18, 19.

We expected changes in functional connectivity across groups to scale with AUD severity. In other words, we expected a progressive increase of change in functional connectivity with increasing AUD severity: light drinkers < heavy drinkers < dependent drinkers. Specifically, we hypothesized that during alcohol cue-exposure (a) functional connectivity between seed regions and the dlPFC would decrease, while (b) functional connectivity between seed regions and the OFC would increase as AUD severity increases. In abstinent alcohol-dependent patients, we expected (c) strengthened functional connectivity between seed regions and the dlPFC compared with nonabstinent dependent drinkers. Finally, we expected (d) functional connectivity change during alcohol cue-exposure to correlate with subjective craving and compulsivity of alcohol use.

2 MATERIAL AND METHODS

2.1 Participants

A total of 96 participants (27 female, 69 male, age 46 ± 11 years [mean ± standard deviation, SD], range 19-65) were included in the investigation. Ten participants belonged to the group of light social alcohol drinkers, eight to nonabstinent heavy drinkers, 13 to nonabstinent alcohol-dependent drinkers, and 65 to recently detoxified, abstinent alcohol-dependent patients. Further sample characteristics are displayed in Table 1. Light drinkers, heavy drinkers, and dependent drinkers were recruited through announcements in a local newspaper. None of them were treatment seeking. Abstinent alcohol-dependent patients were recruited from the addiction clinic of the Department of Addictive Behavior and Addiction Medicine at the Central Institute of Mental Health (ZI) in Mannheim. The sample of untreated alcohol consumers was originally acquired for a study on neural consequences of chronic alcohol use1, 21. Abstinent alcohol-dependent patients were recruited for a study on exposure therapy in alcohol dependence22, 23. All of the participants' data was repurposed in this study.

Eligibility criteria for all groups were: age between 18 and 65 years and normal or corrected-to-normal vision (binocular visual acuity ≥0.8). Patients had to be abstinent for at least three days since detoxification. Exclusion criteria for any of the groups were current use of psychotropic or anticonvulsive medications, other substance dependencies apart from alcohol and/or nicotine, neurological or severe medical illness, or any MRI-exclusion criteria (e.g. metal implants, pacemakers). All inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table S1. Participants completed the Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS)30. Before the fMRI scan, breath alcohol was zero, and none of the participants showed clinically relevant withdrawal symptoms, measured by the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol-Revised (CIWA-Ar)31. All participants provided informed written consent according to the declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee.

2.2 fMRI task

Participants were presented with 13 blocks of alcohol cues and eight blocks of neutral cues. After each block, participants rated the intensity of subjective craving for alcohol on a visual analogue scale from 0 to 100 (0 = no craving at all, 100 = severe craving). One block was presented for 20 seconds and was composed of five stimuli, each presented for four seconds. The total duration of the cue-reactivity task was 12 minutes. The course of the cue-reactivity task is depicted in Figure 1.

Alcohol-related stimuli originated from an alcohol picture series created by the ZI Mannheim and are used in previous studies1, 21-23. Pictures from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS)32 were selected to serve as neutral stimuli and matched to the alcohol pictures for color distribution and complexity. Pictures of nonalcoholic beverages were excluded from the sample of neutral stimuli to avoid the misperception as alcoholic beverage (e.g. perceiving a glass of water as a glass of vodka).

2.3 fMRI acquisition

A 3 Tesla whole-body tomograph (MAGNETOM Trio with TIM technology; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) was used for scanning. T2* weighted echo-planar images (EPI) were recorded with a transversal orientation of 30° clockwise to the anterior commissures-posterior commissures (AC-PC) line. For each subject, 305 images were acquired. The following scan parameters were used: repetition time (TR) = 2.41 seconds, echo time (TE) = 25 ms, flip angle = 80°, number of slices = 42, slice thickness = 2 mm, gap = 1 mm, voxel dimensions = 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, field of view (FOV) = 192 × 192 mm2, 64 × 64 in-plane resolution. An additional T1-weighted three-dimensional MPRAGE data set was acquired to create higher resolution anatomical reference images and to evaluate anatomical abnormalities (192 sagittal slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel dimensions = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 3.03 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°). The cue-reactivity task was viewed with fMRI-compatible goggles using MRI Audio/Video Systems (Resonance Technology Inc., Los Angeles, CA, USA). The Presentations software (version 9.9; Neurobehavioral Systems, INC. Albany, CA, USA) was used to present the task and record behavioral responses of the subjects.

2.4 fMRI preprocessing

The data of this study comprises data from previously published cue-reactivity studies. For reasons of comparability, we used the same preprocessed data including first-level statistics. The subsequent second-level statistics should not differ between SPM8 and SPM12 since the main modifications affect the preprocessing (especially normalization).

Preprocessing of brain imaging data was performed using SPM8 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging at University College London, UK). The first five scans of each participant were excluded from all analyses to avoid artefacts due to magnetic saturation effects. The remaining 300 images were spatially realigned to control for head movements and normalized to a Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI, Quebec, Canada) EPI template. Smoothing was performed using an isotopic Gaussian kernel for group analysis (8-mm full width at half maximum).

3 STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

First-level analyses of the preprocessed fMRI data were performed by modeling the different conditions (alcohol blocks, neutral blocks, and rating periods) as explanatory variables on a voxel-by-voxel basis within the context of the general linear model (GLM) in SPM8. For each participant, the contrast “alcohol > neutral” was modeled as contrast of interest. Individual contrast images were implemented in second-level analyses (one-sample t-test and flexible factorial test). To control for multiple testing, the cluster-size threshold for the imaging analysis that satisfies a family-wise error (FWE) of P < .05 was determined by means of 10 000 iterative Monte Carlo simulations using a whole-brain mask and an automatically conducted estimation of smoothness using the program 3DClustSim (Analysis of Functional NeuroImages, AFNI). For second-level analyses of functional activation, a voxel-wise-threshold of P < .001 and a cluster-extent-threshold of k > 168 voxels were determined.

3.1 Psychophysiological interaction analyses

Psychophysiological interaction analyses were conducted to determine functional connectivity between the seed regions of interest (ROIs) and other brain areas12. The ventral striatum, dorsal striatum, anterior insula, and posterior insula were used as bilateral ROIs. PPI analyses were carried out according to the standard procedure implemented in SPM12. The experimental condition (alcohol vs neutral) was used as psychological variable and the time course of activation in the ROIs as physiological variable. Predefined anatomical masks were used to extract the time series of activation of each ROI. For the ventral striatum, masks from the Nielsen and Hansen database33 were downloaded and converted to a binary mask with a threshold of ≥ 0.25. The dorsal striatum mask was created by subtracting the ventral striatum mask from the putamen/caudate mask of the SPM toolbox Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL)34. The insula was extracted from the AAL atlas and divided into a posterior (y ≤ 0) and an anterior (y ≥ 0) part. Voxel-wise- and cluster-extent thresholds for second-level analyses of the PPI results, as determined with the program 3DClustSim, can be consulted in Table S2. To identify different frontal cortex areas, a classification in Brodmann areas (BA) was used with BA 47 corresponding to the lateral OFC, BA 11 and 12 to the medial OFC, BA 9 and 46 to the dlPFC, and BA 24, 32, and 33 to the ACC35, 36. Planned group comparisons of functional connectivity change were conducted between all groups, respectively.

3.2 Analyses of OCDS and craving scores

To evaluate the questionnaire responses acquired before and during the fMRI session (OCDS and subjective craving), the software IBM Statistical Package of the Social Sciences (SPSS version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used. A descriptive analysis was administered, determining mean, standard deviation, and range of OCDS scores and subjective craving scores for each group. The scores were compared between groups. The nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was administered to compare both OCDS scores and subjective craving scores between groups, since requirements for a univariate analysis of variance were not fulfilled. To observe the respective group contrasts, post-hoc Dunn-Bonferroni tests were conducted.

For correlational analyses between PPI results and questionnaire scores, a linear regression was conducted in SPM12. The contrast images “PPI alcohol > neutral” for each ROI were implemented as criterion in the linear regression. OCDS scores as well as subjective craving scores were used as predictors. Contrast images for both positive and negative associations of functional connectivity strength with either of the questionnaire variables were created. Voxel-wise and cluster-extent thresholds for the correlational analyses, as determined by 3DClustSim, can be consulted in Table S2.

4 RESULTS

4.1 Whole group comparison of cue-induced functional activation

Whole group analyses (n = 96) revealed a main effect of condition on BOLD response in a wide network of brain regions associated with the neural reward system, attention, and object recognition. BOLD response of the prefrontal cortex was increased in the left medial OFC (BA 11), lateral OFC (BA 47), ACC (BA 32), and insula (BA 13) during the presentation of alcohol stimuli compared with neutral stimuli across all participants (see Table S3 and Figure S1).

4.2 Functional connectivity

Whole group PPI analyses (n = 96) revealed greater correlations of neural activation in the alcohol condition compared with the neutral condition between the seed region in the left ventral striatum and bilateral limbic and temporal regions, as well as the bilateral insula and putamen (see Table S4 and Figure S2). A flexible factorial test revealed that abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with heavy drinkers showed reduced cue-induced functional connectivity between the seed regions in the left and right posterior insula and the left mPFC (BA 6, 8; see Table 2(b)). Compared with dependent drinkers, abstinent alcohol-dependent patients showed increased task-related coupling during the alcohol condition between the left ventral striatum and the left ACC (BA 32), dlPFC (BA 9), and mPFC (BA 10), between the right ventral striatum and the bilateral lateral OFC (BA 47), ACC (BA 24, 32), dlPFC (BA 9, 46), insula (BA 13), and mPFC (BA 10), and between the right anterior insula and the bilateral ACC (BA 32), right dlPFC (BA 46), mPFC (BA 10), caudate, and putamen (see Table 2(d) and Figure 2). Reduced cue-induced functional connectivity was observed in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with dependent drinkers between the left anterior insula and the left IFG (BA 44; see Table 2(e)). No significant group differences of functional connectivity change were observed between the groups of light drinkers and heavy drinkers, light drinkers and dependent drinkers, and light drinkers and abstinent alcohol-dependent patients.

4.3 Correlations with questionnaire scores

However, in a post-hoc Dunn-Bonferroni test, none of the group contrasts for subjective craving scores remained significant after adjustments. See Table S5 for all results of the group comparisons.

Regarding subjective craving, negative correlations with cue-induced functional connectivity change were observed between the seed region in the left ventral striatum and the left lateral OFC (47), and the bilateral ACC (BA 24, 32), dlPFC (BA 9), and mPFC (BA 6, 8; see Table 4(a) and Figure 3A). 4.4 Exploratory analyses

To account for the imbalance in sample size of the groups and in order to verify if this imbalance might affect results, we repeated the group comparisons of functional connectivity change and the correlational analyses between functional connectivity change and questionnaire scores with more distributed group sizes. Out of the 65 abstinent alcohol-dependent patients, 21 were selected and matched to heavy drinkers and dependent drinkers based on age and smoking status.

The results of increased functional connectivity between the seed regions and frontal cortex during the presentation of alcohol cues in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with dependent drinkers remained stable. Effect sizes on the neural level are expected to be rather higher than on the subjective level. Hence, for correlational analyses between brain connectivity changes and subjective craving measures (i.e. compulsivity of alcohol use and subjective craving), the effect sizes are expected to be lower than for group comparisons of objective brain connectivity measures. Therefore, correlational analyses were repeated with an exploratory lower threshold in the smaller sample size. The results were found to be stable for OCDS scores and similar by trend for subjective craving scores.

The method and the results of the PPI analyses with a smaller sample size can be consulted in more detail in Supporting Information (Supplementary Methods, Tables S6-S10, and Figures S3-S5).

5 DISCUSSION

The main result of our study was the detection of context-related dynamic adaptations of cue-processing and cognitive control networks in Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD), depending on the amount of current alcohol consumption and the state of development of classical symptoms of dependence, e.g. craving and compulsivity of use. In our data, we found evidence supporting the network hypothesis of cortical brain regions inhibiting and controlling impulsive alcohol urges generated by the mesolimbic reward system. Correspondingly, we found strengthened connectivity of cortical brain regions to the mesolimbic reward system in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with nonabstinent dependent drinkers.

Our findings included increased task-related coupling of ventral striatum and anterior insula seed regions with the dlPFC in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with dependent drinkers during exposure to alcohol-associated stimuli. These observed differences in functional connectivity change even remained stable in exploratory analysis with a smaller sample size. As suggested by Peters and colleagues, the prefrontal cortex exerts a top-down regulation constituting self-control, which is defined as the ability to overcome the urge to select tempting but ultimately inferior decision options37. In light of this assumption, our observation suggests a greater implementation of self-control in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients when confronted with alcohol cues than in dependent drinkers. Our results are in line with the wider literature on changes of resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) and further support Camchong and colleagues' theory of increasing cognitive control over consumption impulses in abstinence8.

Expanding on findings made by Schulte and colleagues29, our study demonstrates that neural activation is not the only measurable functional parameter sensitive to alcohol-associated stimuli which also correlates with clinical measures including subjective craving and severity of alcohol use. Confirming our hypothesis, we observed correlations between functional connectivity change during alcohol cue-exposure and both ratings of subjective craving and OCDS scores. Strengthened cue-induced functional connectivity between ventral striatum, anterior insula, and posterior insula seed regions and the dlPFC was positively associated with OCDS scores. Furthermore, a positive association between task-related functional connectivity change between posterior insula and ACC with OCDS scores was observed. These results were also replicable in the exploratory analyses with a reduced sample size. In line with results from rsFC studies8, 38, our observations support the theory of compensatory mechanisms in abstinent individuals: the implementation of cognitive control over reward-directed behavior is increased during the presentation of alcohol-associated stimuli, especially in individuals with high scores on compulsivity of use, in order to resist actual consumption. As a caveat, it may be possible that the group of abstinent alcohol-dependent patients is, in general, more reflective about the compulsive character of their disorder, leading them to self-report higher OCDS scores. Dependent drinkers and heavy drinkers, who neither receive nor seek treatment, may not be as aware of the severity of their consumption behavior and therefore rate the compulsive character of their alcohol use lower on the OCDS. This bias of introspection must be considered when interpreting the results regarding compulsivity of alcohol use.

Cue-induced functional connectivity change between posterior insula and medial OFC correlated positively with mean ratings of subjective craving after viewing alcohol stimuli, in line with observations of cue-induced functional connectivity change in smokers16. The evidence suggests a strengthened connection between interoceptive processes in the insula and reward value evaluation and reward-related choices in the medial OFC35 when craving is stronger. The causality of this relationship, however, needs further investigation. Observed negative associations between task-related functional connectivity change of the ventral striatum and lateral OFC, ACC, and dlPFC with subjective craving scores are comparable to observations of rsFC studies4, 39, 40. The lateral OFC is associated with punishment evaluation and behavioral guidance4, while abnormal connectivity between striatum and dlPFC was associated with impairments of flexibly adapting behavior to changes of reward contingencies41. Results suggest a reduced ability to update reward value and change behavior accordingly when craving is high. Even though craving is a subjective measure and is therefore more affected by small sample sizes due to smaller effect sizes, the observed correlations were found as a trend in the reduced sample. Nonetheless, considering this lack of replicability, the results of associations between functional connectivity change and subjective craving should be interpreted with caution.

Contrary to our hypotheses, no significant group differences of cue-induced functional connectivity change were observed between light drinkers, heavy drinkers, and dependent drinkers. Past research reporting differences of connectivity between the theorized brain areas comparing substance abusers and healthy controls conducted analyses of rsFC instead of psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analyses4-6. Changes of functional connectivity in dependent drinkers compared with healthy controls may only be observable at rest. According to our findings, functional connectivity change implemented in response to alcohol-associated stimuli does not differ between light drinkers, heavy drinkers, and dependent drinkers. Our findings suggest that functional connectivity change only differs in abstinent alcohol-dependent individuals in the specific context of alcohol cue-exposure in order to resist subjective craving. However, the lack of differences between light drinkers, heavy drinkers, and dependent drinkers may also be due to the small group sizes.

The results of this investigation support the theory of neural compensatory mechanisms in abstinent AUD individuals. Increased task-related coupling between ventral striatum, anterior insula, and dlPFC in abstinent alcohol-dependent patients compared with nonabstinent dependent drinkers during the exposure to alcohol-associated stimuli suggests an increased implementation of cognitive control in abstinent individuals when confronted with alcohol cues. Negative correlations were observed for subjective craving and task-related functional connectivity change between ventral striatum and lateral OFC, indicating a reduced ability to adjust behavior according to the severity of the negative effects of alcohol use with increased subjective craving. Compulsivity of alcohol use and task-related functional connectivity change between ventral striatum, anterior insula, posterior insula, and dlPFC correlated positively. The implementation of cognitive control appears to be increased especially when compulsivity of use is high.

6 CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Despite neural changes indicating an increased implementation of cognitive control, our results confirm that alcohol stimuli remain highly salient and continue to provoke excessive craving in abstinence. The results stress the importance of targeting cue-provoked craving and compulsivity of use in AUD treatment. Prevalent treatments to reduce cue-provoked craving in AUD individuals are cue-exposure based extinction trainings22 and opioid antagonists42, 43. However, the available treatment methods are still inefficient in completely solving the problem of AUD. Understanding the neural correlates underlying AUD, abstinence, and relapse, especially during the acute exposure to alcohol cues, is essential for developing more effective treatment methods. The neural correlates determined in this investigation should be included in the development and evaluation of more effective treatment methods, as they were determined in the context of acute cue-exposure instead of at rest.

Further research is needed to investigate whether the observed neural adaptations remain over time or whether they degenerate. A compensatory increase of cognitive control over reward related behavior may degenerate over time, if excessive craving and compulsivity of use decrease with prolonged abstinence or through effective treatment. Current theory suggests that the attribution of increased salience to substances of abuse and related stimuli is permanent, which is why abstinent individuals remain at a high risk for relapse in the presence of alcohol cues44.

7 LIMITATIONS

Only small sample sizes were available for the groups of light drinkers (n = 10), heavy drinkers (n = 8), and dependent drinkers (n = 13). Small effects may therefore not be detectable. Replications of this study should include larger sample sizes that are equal across all groups to increase statistical power and avoid contortions of the results in favor of one group. To further explore one of our main findings of strengthened connectivity in abstinent state, future studies should also look at the association between length of abstinence and strength of connectivity. Unfortunately, our study design was not suitable for this question, because patients were included with a very narrow range of abstinent days (see Table 1) resulting in low variance in abstinence length.

A limitation of the administered PPI analyses is the lack of causality. To determine directionality of influence of one brain region over another during the presentation of alcohol stimuli, an analysis of effective connectivity should be administered.

In this study, we carried out eight whole brain PPI analyses (four seed ROI, bilateral). Within each analysis, multiple testing was controlled for by the calculated combined voxel-wise and cluster-extent thresholds. However, when interpreting the results, it must be kept in mind that between the eight separate PPI analyses, multiple testing was not controlled for.

Lastly, the sample of abstinent alcohol-dependent patients in this study was abstinent for only seven days on average. Dennis and colleagues stated that from a sample of alcohol-dependent patients maintaining abstinence for less than a year, only 36% will reach long-term abstinence. Significant neural differences were observed between relapsers and abstainers9, 25,46. In our investigation, treatment outcome was not considered. This may cause unreliable results regarding the group of abstinent alcohol-dependent patients; interpretations should be regarded with caution.
