Abstract

The compulsion to seek and use heroin is frequently driven by stress and craving during drug-cue exposure. Although previous neuroimaging studies have indicated that craving is mediated by increased prefrontal cortex activity, it remains unknown how heroin administration modulates the prefrontal cortex response. This study examines the acute effects of heroin on brain function in heroin-maintained patients. Using a crossover, double-blind, placebo-controlled design, 27 heroin-maintained patients performed functional magnetic resonance imaging 20 minutes after the administration of heroin or placebo (saline) while drug-related and neutral stimuli were presented. Images were processed and analysed with statistical parametric mapping. Plasma concentrations of heroin and its main metabolites were assessed using high-performance liquid chromatography. Region of interest analyses showed a drug-related cue-associated blood-oxygen-level-dependent activation in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) in heroin-dependent patients during both treatment conditions (heroin and placebo). This activation of the OFC was significantly higher after heroin than after placebo administration. These findings may indicate the importance of OFC activity for impulse control and decision-making after regular heroin administration and may emphasize the benefit of the heroin-assisted treatment in heroin dependence.

Introduction

Drug addiction is a chronically relapsing disorder characterized by compulsive drug use and loss of control over drug intake (Leshner 1997). The compulsion to use drugs is frequently driven by stress and craving. Animal models of craving involve reinstatement of drug seeking following extinction by the drugs themselves, by cues linked to the drug and or by exposure to stressors (Shaham et al. 2003). Drug-related cues acquire incentive-motivational value, and evoke expectations of drug availability and memories of past drug euphoria. It is well known that exposure to drug-related cues results in an increase in stress responses and drug craving in heroin addicts (Walter et al. 2008, 2011).

Clinical research has demonstrated that opioid maintenance with regular oral administration of methadone produces favourable treatment retention and outcome in heroin dependence. However, some heroin-dependent individuals refuse methadone maintenance, while others respond poorly to treatment (Strain & Stitzer 2006). Therefore, alternative pharmacotherapies have been tested, including the prescription of pharmaceutical heroin (diacetylmorphine) for heroin-dependent patients. In previous research, the heroin-assisted treatment was consistently found to be an effective treatment for severe heroin dependence (Van den Brink et al. 2003; Haasen et al. 2007; Oviedo-Joekes et al. 2009).

Intravenously injected heroin is rapidly converted to 6-monacetylmorphine (6AM) and to morphine. There is evidence that the central effects of heroin and its main psychoactive metabolites—6AM and morphine—are mediated by the two main receptor subtypes, the μ and κ opioid receptors (Pfeiffer et al. 1982). Short-term administration of heroin produces euphoria, sedation and a feeling of tranquillity. Repeated administration rapidly produces tolerance and physical dependence (Cami & Farré 2003).

Over the past decade, structural (sMRI) and functional (fMRI) magnetic resonance imaging methods have been employed to identify the anatomical and neurofunctional alterations in heroin users. sMRI studies examining heroin users have consistently demonstrated decreases in grey matter density in the prefrontal and temporal cortex (Lyoo et al. 2006; Liu et al. 2009; Yuan et al. 2009). In fMRI studies, an increased blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) response has been found in the prefrontal areas during drug-related cues. Drug-related cues activated the inferior frontal cortex compared with water-related cues (Xiao et al. 2006), and drug-related cues activated different prefrontal areas compared with neutral cues (Yang et al. 2009). These results confirm the critical role of the prefrontal cortex in drug craving of heroin addicts.

The initial pharmaco-fMRI studies investigating the acute effects of opioid administration on brain function showed a reduced BOLD response in the insula, amygdala, hippocampus and ventral tegmental area during drug-related cues after methadone (Langleben et al. 2008) and buprenorphine administration (Mei, Zhang & Xiao 2010). Neither study included a control group and therefore did not compare the brain function of addicted patients and healthy controls.

In general, it has been hypothesized that drug seeking of addicted subjects is mainly mediated by drug-induced changes in prefrontal areas, and more specifically in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) (Schoenbaum, Roesch & Stalnaker 2006). It is well known that the OFC receives projections from the ventral tegmental area and the nucleus accumbens, which is considered to be the target for the reinforcing effects of addictive drugs. Moreover, the OFC is known to be able to integrate information from various limbic regions and to modulate the response of these brain regions to drug administration (Volkow & Fowler 2000). The OFC has been proposed to be involved in sensory integration, in representing the affective value of reinforcers, in inhibitory control and in decision-making (Goldstein & Volkow 2002; Kringelbach 2005). Indeed, there is some evidence showing that this particular function of the OFC is disrupted by exposure to addictive drugs, leading to maladaptive and impulsive decision-making in drug addicts (Schoenbaum & Shaham 2008).

In the present study, we used fMRI to study heroin-maintained patients while they performed a drug-related cue task following administration of either heroin or placebo. We expected drug-induced changes in the OFC response to drug-related cues after heroin administration, in comparison with neutral stimuli.

Patients and Methods

Design

A randomized, placebo-controlled, crossover clinical trial was conducted over two sessions for heroin-dependent patients. The patients were scanned with fMRI twice, with a 1-week interval. Following simple randomization procedures, they were randomly assigned to one of two injected substances (heroin versus placebo/saline). One patient group first received heroin before the experiment, whereas the second patient group first received saline. One week later, they received the other substance before the experiment. All patients were informed that they would receive heroin and placebo or placebo and heroin consecutively. For ethical reasons, healthy controls were included for placebo administration only.

Sample

Twenty-seven heroin-dependent and opioid-maintained outpatients were recruited from the Centre of Substance Use Disorders of the Psychiatric Hospital of the University of Basel (Switzerland). The inclusion criteria were: age greater than 18 years, history of intravenous heroin use, having been on current stable heroin-assisted treatment for at least 6 months and unchanged heroin dose during the previous 3 months. Exclusion criteria were a positive alcohol breathalyzer test and an additional physical disease or psychiatric disorder, including other co-morbid severe substance dependence.

When heroin-maintained patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria, the history of heroin and other illicit substance use was assessed with the semi-structured interview according to International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision research criteria. Patients were told to abstain from illicit drug use other than prescribed heroin for the duration of the study, from alcohol intake for 72 hours and from tobacco consumption for 2 hours before scanning. Nevertheless, nine patients (and five controls) were tested positive for cannabis and 13 patients for cocaine at one or both points of the measurement. The last regular heroin injection was 12 hours before the experiment.

The healthy controls were carefully screened, using a semi-structured clinical interview, in order to exclude psychiatric or physical illness or a family history of psychiatric illness. Participants who had ever used any other psychotropic drug, who consumed more than 20 g alcohol per day, or who had any psychiatric, neurologic, or severe medical illness history, were also excluded. Healthy controls were recruited from the general population by advertisement in the same geographical area.

After the study had been completely described to the subjects, written informed consent was obtained. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and registered with http://www.clinicaltrials.gov/ (ID NCT01174927).

Procedure

The study procedure has previously been described in more detail (Schmidt et al. 2013). Briefly, after completion of the baseline behavioural measurement, heroin-dependent patients received either their dose of prescribed heroin in 5 ml or the same dose of saline, through an indwelling intravenous catheter over a period of 30 seconds. Healthy controls were injected with 5 ml saline. The Swiss Federal Office of Public Health provided heroin as the hydrochloride salt. This was dissolved in sterile water on site and aspirated into a syringe, adapted to the evacuated infusion system. The patients and the experimenter were blind to the administered substance. The concentrations of heroin and its metabolites were obtained at baseline and 3, 10 and 60 minutes after the patients had received their injection. Twenty minutes after the injection, all patients underwent the MRI procedure. After scanning, and 60 minutes after the injection, patients and controls fulfilled the second behavioural measurement before they received heroin or saline.

Self-reports

Craving was assessed using the 45-item Heroin Craving Questionnaire (Tiffany 1999), which measures positive and negative aspects of craving on five theory-derived 9-item scales. The corresponding scale measures the desire to use heroin.

Bioanalytical measurements

Urine samples were analysed by the Clinical Chemical Laboratory of the University Hospital Basel for the presence of alcohol, amphetamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, cannabinoids, cocaine, ecstasy, methadone and opiates using enzyme immunoassays.

Plasma levels of heroin, and the main psychoactive metabolites 6AM and morphine, were measured in venous ammonium-heparinized plasma and assessed using high-performance liquid chromatography on a 125 × 2 mm i.d. Nucleosil 50 C-8 ec column with a particle size of 5 µm and a 8 × 3 mm i.d. precolumn packed with Nucleosil 120 C-8 and a particle size of 3 µm followed by diode-array detection. Sample preparation and instrumental conditions were as described previously in detail (Bourquin et al. 1999).

Image acquisition of structural and functional MRI data

For anatomical imaging, a three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition with gradient echo sequence was applied with 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 isotropic resolution and with inversion time of 1000 milliseconds, repetition time of 8 milliseconds and echo time of 3.4 milliseconds. All scans were screened for gross radiological abnormalities by an experienced neuroradiologist. We acquired the drug cue-related task elicited images on the same three T scanner (Siemens Magnetom Verio, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany), using an echo planar sequence with a repetition time of 2.5 seconds, echo time of 28 milliseconds, matrix size of 76 × 76, 38 slices with 0.5 mm interslice gap, providing a resolution of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3 and a field of view 228 × 228 cm2. In total, 126 volumes were acquired.

Functional MRI activation paradigm: drug cue-related task

The drug cues paradigm was performed with two stimuli conditions: heroin-related and neutral images. The heroin-related images were 24 non-standardized photographs showing themes related to heroin use, like heroin injection, preparation of heroin and typical usage situations. As neutral stimuli, 24 standardized images (Lang, Bradley & Cuthbert 1997) were used, such as landscapes, plants, animals and household objects. In each imaging session, all 48 images were presented in a pseudo-randomized, event-related fashion. The protocol was programmed and scanner synchronized with E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Sharpsburg, PA, USA). Each stimulus contained a 500 milliseconds display of a heroin-related or neutral image, followed by the presentation of a 1500 milliseconds display of a crosshair graphic. The stimuli were separated by a variable interval form 0 to 18 seconds during which the crosshair graphic was also displayed.

Image processing and analysis

Structural MRI

Structural scans were preprocessed using the Voxel-Based Morphometry toolbox (VBM 8; http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/vbm8/), implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM 8; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). We modulated the segmented tissue maps of grey matter with the Jacobian determinants from the spatial normalization, in order to correct for volume changes. Finally, we smoothed the modulated GM images with an 8 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Statistical significance was assessed at the cluster level, using non-stationary random field theory (Hayasaka et al. 2004). The first step of this cluster-level inference strategy consisted of identifying spatially contiguous voxels at a threshold of P < 0.01, without correction (cluster-forming threshold) (Petersson et al. 1999). Finally, a family-wise error (FWE)-corrected cluster-extent threshold of P < 0.05 was defined in order to infer statistical significance. A voxel-wise whole brain analysis of the modulated volumes was performed with a general linear model (GLM) and two sample t-tests for the following contrasts:

GMheroin < GMcontrol and GMheroin > GMcontrol. Age, gender and cocaine use were used as covariates.

Functional MRI data acquisition and analysis

We analysed fMRI data using the SPM8 software package. All volumes were realigned to the first volume, corrected for motion artefacts, mean adjusted by proportional scaling, normalized into standard stereotactic space (MNI template), and smoothed using a 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. A voxel-wise analysis of normalized and smoothed images was performed with a first level GLM. For model specification, onset times of drug-related cues and neutral stimuli were convolved with a canonical haemodynamic response function. We also modelled time and dispersion derivatives and used a high-pass filter of 128 seconds to correct for slow signal drifts.

Second level analyses were performed using a GLM with beta-images of first level BOLDdrugcues and BOLDneutral as input data. To address our a priori hypothesis, and for explanatory reasons, we used whole brain analyses. On the basis of our priori hypotheses, we employed the explicit mask of the OFC (right and left hemisphere), taken from the WFU PickAtlas (http://fmri.wfubmc.edu/software/pickatlas) and the Individual Brain Atlases using SPM atlas (OFC = Frontal_Mid_Orb + Rectus). In our statistical models, we included age, sex, cocaine use and scan order (order of heroin and placebo application) as covariates. A cluster-forming threshold of P < 0.01 and an FWE-corrected threshold of P < 0.05 were defined in order to infer statistical significance.

Firstly, to calculate drug-related effects for patient and control groups, we performed one sample t-tests with a BOLDdrug cues > BOLDneutral contrast for each group. Furthermore, we used a repeated measures (within-subject) ANOVA to examine differences between treatment conditions. We used F-tests to investigate the interaction effects of treatment (heroin and placebo) and cues (drug cues and neutral stimuli). T-contrasts were used to specify the direction of treatment effect by the contrasts Heroin > Placebo and Heroin < Placebo. We used the eigenvariate function in SPM8 to extract mean drug-related cue-associated changes in the BOLD signal within the OFC mask.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 19, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyse behavioural measures and drug levels. Data were analysed by means of unpaired and paired t-tests for continuous variables or χ2 tests for discrete variables. All statistical tests were considered significant at a two-tailed level of P < 0.05. Pearson r correlations were calculated between the extracted change in BOLD signal related to drug cues and clinical data (duration of heroin dependence, heroin dose, desire to use heroin).

Results

The mean age of the heroin-maintained group (19 male, eight female) was 41.1 years. The daily heroin dose ranged from 30 to 500 mg. The mean age of the healthy controls (14 male, six female) was 40.3 years. The groups of patients and controls did not differ in age, gender, handedness or cannabis consumption. Patients used more cocaine and tobacco and were less often employed than controls. Table 1 gives an overview of the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample.

Drug levels of heroin and its psychoactive metabolites

After heroin injection, the peak plasma concentrations of heroin were highest at 3 minutes. Morphine—the main psychoactive metabolite—was detectable in all patients and at all sampling time points. Morphine exhibited a much longer elimination half-life than the acetylated compounds, so that the plasma concentrations of morphine declined more slowly than the concentrations of heroin and 6AM. At 3, 10 and 60 minutes, the mean concentrations of morphine were 529 (SD = 726), 334 (SD = 156) and 224 ng/ml (SD = 92), respectively. The plasma profiles of heroin, morphine and 6AM are depicted in Fig. 1.

Effects of heroin on craving

Heroin craving decreased in heroin-dependent patients after heroin (t = 8.47, d.f. = 26, P < 0.0001) and craving increased after placebo administration (t = −2.80, d.f. = 26, P = 0.009) (Fig. 2).

Whole brain grey matter volumes in patients and controls

In comparison to healthy controls, patients had significantly less grey matter in the area of the OFC including the right medial frontal gyrus and right middle frontal gyrus (P < 0.01, FWE corrected) (Table 2, Fig. 3a). There was no significant correlation between the grey matter density of extracted OFC values and the duration of heroin use (r = −0.24, P = 0.22) or the heroin dose (r = −0.17, P = 0.40).

Effects of drug-related cues on whole brain activation

After placebo administration, drug-related cues were associated with left hemispheric activation in the lateral occipital cortex (P < 0.001, FWE corrected), in the medial frontal cortex (P < 0.001, FWE corrected), and in the inferior frontal gyrus (P = 0.014, FWE corrected). After heroin administration, drug-related cues were not associated with a significantly increased BOLD response. A non-significant cluster was found in the frontal medial cortex (P = 0.084, FWE corrected). In the control group, drug-related cues were—in contrast to neutral stimuli—associated with an increased BOLD response in the left lateral occipital cortex (P < 0.001, FWE corrected) and the post-central gyrus (P = 0.002, FWE corrected).

Effects of drug-related cues on OFC activation

In contrast to neutral stimuli, drug-related cues were associated with significant BOLD responses in the OFC after placebo and after heroin administration (P < 0.01, FWE corrected) (Table 3, Fig. 3b). In the control group, drug-related cues were not associated with a significantly increased BOLD response within the OFC.

Discussion

In this study, we used sMRI and fMRI to investigate how heroin modulates brain function in heroin-dependent patients during presentation of drug-related and neutral stimuli.

We found that heroin-dependent patients had reduced grey matter volumes in the OFC compared with healthy controls. This finding is consistent with previous results showing volumetric reductions in prefrontal brain areas in patients with regular opioid use (Wang et al. 2012). In previous studies, the volume negatively correlated with the duration of heroin consumption (Yuan et al. 2009; Qiu et al. 2013), suggesting a positive association between long-term heroin addiction and grey matter atrophy in prefrontal brain regions. These findings support the hypothesis that repeated drug consumption induces neuroadaptive changes with detectable regional brain loss in brain neurotransmitter systems of the prefrontal brain areas (Volkow, Fowler & Wang 2003; Denier et al. 2013). However, we did not find this negative correlation between grey matter volume and duration of heroin use in our sample, which implies that also pre-existing differences could be possible, or that other substances of abuse may be associated with reduced grey matter volumes in OFC in heroin addicts. Frontal grey matter regions were described as being particularly vulnerable in chronic alcohol-dependent patients (Bühler & Mann 2011), and more specifically, there is evidence that chronic cocaine exposure alters structural and neurophysiological markers of the OFC function (Lucantonio et al. 2012).

We found that OFC activity increased in heroin-dependent patients after acute administration of a single heroin dose compared with placebo while processing drug-related cues. The prefrontal complex activation has been described during drug-related cues (Yang et al. 2009), and was identified as the critical brain region for drug craving (Koob & Volkow 2010). However, we found that OFC activity increased and that drug craving decreased after heroin administration. Recently, it was reported that changes in craving scores following drug-cue exposure were associated with mesolimbic responses and not with changes in OFC activity (Li et al. 2012), which indicates that OFC activity may not be specifically associated with craving changes.

In general, addicts will experience reduced craving, as long as the automaticity of drug use is not disrupted (Tiffany & Carter 1998). Indeed, when the OFC is mainly characterized as using associative information to project the future and as using the value of perceived and expected outcome to guide decisions (Schoenbaum et al. 2006), the increase in OFC activity towards drug-related cues indicates that non-automatic cognitive processes and not the desire to use drugs will be processed after heroin administration. Our results indicate that regular heroin treatment may acutely shifts automatic processes to higher-order cognitive functions including the ability to control and inhibit drug-taking behaviour. These findings provide important clinical implications as a heroin-assisted treatment is currently used in several countries with promising outcomes (Oviedo-Joekes et al. 2009).

Recently, an increased fMRI response to drug-related cues was also found in the medial frontal gyrus after injection of the opioid antagonist naltrexone (Langleben et al. 2014). Although it is somewhat surprising that administration of μ-opioid receptor antagonists (naltrexone) as well as μ-opioid receptor agonists (heroin) lead to prefrontal activation during drug-related cues, it is well known that both substances are effective pharmacological treatments, leading to significant reductions in drug craving and illicit heroin use (Blanken et al. 2012; Krupitsky et al. 2013). We can argue that both substances activate the prefrontal cortex—especially the OFC—during drug-cue exposure by different neuronal pathways and may help heroin addicts in self-control and in making decisions to avoid automatic addictive behaviours that culminate in later drug use.

Moreover, in our previous studies, we could also demonstrate decreases in anxiety and stress hormones after heroin administration compared with placebo (Gerber et al. 2012; Walter et al. 2013). This dampening effect of heroin on negative emotions—including craving—highlights the acute emotional regulatory effect of heroin administration and suggests that heroin and other opioids may contribute to normalization of the impaired emotional processing and emphasizes the benefits of regular opioid substitution. One important clinical difference between illegal heroin use and opioid substitution is the regular and controlled administration of opioids in a stable treatment setting including the reduction of withdrawal symptoms (Strain & Stitzer 2006).

Regarding the fMRI data, we also found a strong placebo effect on the activation of prefrontal brain areas in patients. It is known that both opioids and placebo lead to increased blood flow in brain regions known to be rich in opioid receptors (Petrovic et al. 2002). These placebo effects may have overlapped here with the acute heroin effect in heroin-dependent patients. Moreover, we examined heroin-dependent patients who were on regular and stable opioid dose. Thus, one single heroin dose may not have as strong effects in these patients as in opioid naïve individuals.

As in previous pharmacological fMRI studies, the sample sizes were modest, as these randomized, placebo-controlled design studies are logistically demanding. Moreover, the healthy control group were not given heroin for ethical reasons. A further caveat is that there is a considerably within-subject difference between heroin dosages. However, our aim was to use the individually prescribed dose of heroin that would be associated with significant effects on neurocognitive function while having as low a risk as possible of severe adverse events.

It should be noted that our patients were recruited from a population that mainly consisted of individuals with long-standing multi-substance use. Although this study was conducted within the heroin-assisted treatment programme, the problem of concurrent use of other drugs is virtually inevitable. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that this may have influenced the results. However, we controlled for these effects in our models and cocaine use did not significantly influence the results. Moreover, because most patients were no longer blind for the second trial, there could be a learning and expectation bias effect to account for the results of the second trial. We controlled for the order of substance administration and did not find a significant influence of the random order of heroin or placebo administration.

To conclude, this is the first study to examine the acute drug-cue related neural effects of heroin in a population of heroin-maintained patients. It establishes that one heroin dose leads to functional activation in the OFC during drug-related cue exposure. These findings may indicate the significance of the OFC activity in avoiding automatic addictive behaviours and may emphasize the benefit of the heroin-assisted treatment. However, future fMRI studies are needed to understand the interplay between acute effects of heroin administration and the long-term effects of heroin-assisted treatment for heroin-dependent individuals.
