Abstract

Aims

To estimate the magnitude of cannabis cue–reactivity responses in people who regularly use cannabis.

Methods

EMBASE, PUBMED, PsycINFO, PsycEXTRA and Project CORK were searched for within-subject comparison studies published between January 2000 and December 2014. Eligible studies compared regular cannabis users' subjective craving or psychophysiological responding when exposed to cannabis cues to their responses to neutral cues and/or to their responses during a baseline assessment. Eligible studies presented original data and were written in English. Fourteen studies met inclusion criteria, but data were not available for two. The 12 included studies contained 393 participants (12–97 participants per study; mean age range: 16.4–32.6 years). Standardized effects sizes were computed for heart rate, skin conductance, skin temperature, blood pressure, event-related potentials (Pz P300) and self-reported craving (Visual Analogue Scales and Marijuana Craving Questionnaire) and synthesized using a multivariate meta-regression model.

Results

On average, cannabis users experienced moderate cue–reactivity, as determined by comparisons with baseline conditions [d = 0.59, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.43, 0.74, P < 0.001] and with neutral cue comparisons (d = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.32, 0.62, P < 0.001). This model, which excluded studies at a greater than low risk of bias and included PzP300 studies as a moderator (due to having larger effect sizes), had a moderately low level of heterogeneity of effect size (I2 = 45.6%, Q = 56.97, P = 0.003) and showed no evidence of publication bias. Cue–reactivity was most noticeable (large effect size) when assessed by Pz P300 waves (d = 1.71, 95% CI = 0.77, 2.64, P = 0.0003; I2 = 86%, Q = 36.30, P < 0.001) and least noticeable (trivial effect size) when examining heart rate (d = 0.14, 95% CI = −0.34, 0.62, P = 0.58; I2 = 80%, Q = 20.0, P < 0.001). These subgroup models demonstrated high heterogeneity of effect size.

Conclusions

Regular cannabis users experience moderate to extremely intense cue–reactivity, such that their attentional biases towards cannabis cues are much stronger than their perceptions of craving for the drug.

Introduction

Craving for substances has long been held to be triggered by cues that have been conditioned to substances and the effects of pleasure or relief that occur after consumption 1. The combination of these conditioned responses and related attentional biases and craving is termed ‘cue–reactivity’. Greater cue–reactivity can increase the risk of relapse to substance misuse 2, 3, giving it significance as a target for treatment. Some theorists take the conditioning argument further, contending that the substance use itself can become automated 1-3, although others emphasize the continued importance of controlled processing as a factor in most substance use—and, indeed, in the maintenance and intensification of substance-related desires 4, 5. Regardless of the relative role that is given to automatic and controlled processes in craving and substance use, modern theories continue to show a consensus on the critical role of conditioning in the initiation of desires and habitual responses 4, 5.

Researchers have studied the ability of cannabis paraphernalia to control behaviour using the cue–reactivity paradigm; however, no one has attempted to consolidate this research systematically and assess the magnitude of cannabis cue–reactivity across studies. Carter & Tiffany's 6 seminal meta-analysis on cue–reactivity to alcohol, opiate, cocaine and nicotine cues found that psychophysiological measures demonstrated less cue-specificity than self-report measures. Small effect sizes were found for heart rate [d = 0.26 (0.17–0.35)], skin conductance [d = 0.40 (0.30–0.49)] and skin temperature [d = −0.24 (−0.35–0.13)], while a large effect size was found for self-reported craving [d = 0.92 (0.84–1.0)]. Carter & Tiffany 6 hypothesized that this pattern of responding may have occurred due to psychophysiological measures being associated with more noise. Physiological responses may be the result of many factors, some of which are unrelated to the drug cues (e.g. movement during the experiment, anxiety associated with research participation). Conversely, self-report measures are more limited to participants' drug desire. Besides calling for more research examining the discrepancy between psychophysiological responding and self-report, Carter & Tiffany 6 recommended that future cue–reactivity studies assess factors that may moderate findings. Such moderators noted were cue presentation order, cue type, control conditions, type of participant and duration of abstinence.

In a short narrative review of 10 cannabis cue–reactivity studies, Filbey & DeWitt 7 concluded that craving elicited by cannabis cues is similar to cue-elicited cravings for other drugs of abuse in that it has subjective and physiological components; however, the authors noted their inability to assess the effects of abstinence on cue-elicited craving. Craving is one aspect of cannabis withdrawal 8. Unlike cue-elicited craving, which is a phasic, short-lived response, the physiological component of craving during withdrawal is a tonic, unconditioned response that peaks and decays gradually over days 9. Cannabis cues may be more salient when individuals are experiencing withdrawal craving and could thus elicit greater reactivity. Conversely, if withdrawal craving is high, cue–reactivity could be limited due to ceiling effects.

Based on the above-mentioned work, we conducted the present meta-analytical study to integrate data quantitatively from experimental studies assessing regular cannabis users' degree of subjective and psychophysiological reactivity to cannabis cues in comparison to baseline and neutral cue conditions. Our aims were to determine the degree of reactivity that cannabis users experience when presented with cannabis cues, and whether the degree of reactivity differs as a function of comparison condition (baseline versus neutral cue) and measure category (self-report craving versus psychophysiological). Lastly, we aimed to examine if certain sample characteristics (e.g. mean age, proportion male) and study characteristics (e.g. type of cues, cue presentation order) influence the degree of cue–reactivity.

Methods

Search procedures and selection criteria

Following a review protocol (unpublished, available from the first author), we searched five electronic databases (EMBASE, PUBMED, PsycINFO, PsycEXTRA and Project CORK) to identify relevant full-text articles published between January 2000 and December 2014. Key search terms were (‘cannabis’ OR ‘marijuana’) AND (‘cue–reactivity’ OR ‘conditioned response’ OR ‘craving’). Database searches were limited to years 2000–14 and human studies published in English (when possible). We also assessed reference lists for additional papers. This search identified 712 records, 323 of which were duplicates. Of the 394 available records, an independent review of the titles and abstracts performed by two authors demonstrated that 367 papers were not eligible. Thus, two authors assessed 27 full-text papers independently for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Studies were eligible if they: (a) examined regular cannabis users; (b) exposed cannabis users to both cannabis cues and neutral cues, or in the absence of neutral cues included a baseline assessment; (c) examined subjective craving or psychophysiological responding; (d) presented original data; and (e) had been written in English. Studies were excluded if (a) they only assessed behavioural (e.g. reaction times) or neuropsychological responses (e.g. fMRI bold responses); (b) included an intervention designed to impact cue–reactivity and did not have data unaffected by the intervention; or (c) if only single words were used to induce cue–reactivity (e.g. Stroop task). Fourteen papers were eligible for inclusion. Figure 1 presents the diagram for study selection informed by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines 10.

Data extraction

Two individuals extracted data independently from eligible studies and entered the data into a structured Excel spreadsheet. The indices extracted from each study included: sample size, age, proportion male, length of use, length of abstinence, type of user, desire to quit, psychological comorbidities, cue exposure technique, outcome measure and outcome. We resolved extraction discrepancies through discussion. When we could not obtain data from a published paper, we asked authors to provide it. Indices with more than 50% missing data are not provided in this report (e.g. desire to quit). Additionally, two studies were excluded at this step because their authors were unable to provide data necessary for computing effect sizes and variance estimates 11, 12.

Quality assessment

Each study's methodological quality was assessed independently by two people using Kmet, Lee & Cook's 13 Checklist for Assessing the Quality of Quantitative Studies. This quality assessment tool allows for variation in experimental designs by utilizing a three-point scoring system (not applicable, no = 0, partial = 1 and yes = 2). Three criteria were not applicable to the types of study included in this review (random allocation of participants, blinding of researchers, blinding of participants) and, thus, total quality scores could range from 0 to 22. Scores 16 and higher were deemed to be at low risk of bias. Eighty-six per cent agreement on study quality was achieved between coders. See Appendix S1 for the list of items that initially produced discrepancies. Item discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We used meta-analytical techniques to evaluate data from eligible studies. For each study, standardized within-subjects effect size estimates [i.e. the difference between responding to the cannabis cues and the neutral cues (or baseline condition)] were calculated using an unbiased estimate of the population effect size δ and its variance (see Appendix S2 for more detail). When t was not available, F was converted to t ( ). All effect size values are presented in the desired direction, such that positive values reflect more reactivity to cannabis cues compared with neutral cues or a baseline assessment.

Meta-analyses were performed for the overall data and for subgroups using the R package metafor 14. When analyses included multiple effect sizes from the same study, a multivariate meta-analysis was conducted with two conditions (i.e. cannabis cue–reactivity in comparison to neutral cues and a baseline condition) as a moderator and random effects for the intercept and slope for each study to account for intrastudy dependence 15. The inclusion of each random effect was determined by the likelihood ratio test and Akaike's information criterion (ACC). We used random-effects models as the included studies were a sample of all possible studies. For the overall meta-analysis, we fitted one model with the two conditions as the only moderator. Another overall model was fitted that included the two comparison conditions as a moderator, as well as other theoretically and methodologically relevant variables that were consistently available (mean centred age, mean centred proportion of male participants, more/less than 24-hour abstinence, exposure presentation order and cue type). We conducted subgroup meta-analyses for self-reported craving, skin conductance, heart rate and Pz300 amplitude, as at least three studies assessed these outcomes. We used a narrative synthesis approach to capture data that was not eligible for the subgroup meta-analysis (blood pressure and skin temperature).

We assessed residual heterogeneity in the overall meta-analysis and in the subgroup analyses with the Q and the I2 statistics. I2 is a transformation of Q that, unlike Q, is not dependent upon the number of studies analysed 16. Conventionally, an I2 of 0, 25, 50 and 75% indicates no, low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively 17. We conducted sensitivity analyses for the overall meta-analysis by including and excluding studies potentially at a high risk of bias (quality scores less than 16). Additionally, we assessed small study bias, such as publication bias, using funnel plots and the rank correlation test. In accordance with Sterne and colleagues' 18 recommendation, tests for funnel plot asymmetry were not conducted when fewer than 10 effect sizes were examined due to insufficient power for distinguishing chance from real asymmetry.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Table 1 summarizes participants' characteristics and design features of the included studies. Sample sizes ranged from 12 to 97 (n = 393), with five of the 12 studies including 16 or fewer participants. Mean sample ages ranged from 16.4 to 32.6 years, and the majority of the samples were dominated by male participants. Seven studies classified participants by how often they used cannabis (at least once per week to daily use), while five established that participants met criteria for cannabis dependence. One study did not require abstinence prior to cue exposure, while five studies required 4–12 hours of abstinence and the remaining six studies required 24 hours or more of abstinence. Four studies presented cues in a fixed order, while another four studies counterbalanced cues, and three studies presented cues in a random order. One study did not include control cues and only compared cannabis cues to a baseline condition. Cannabis cues included actual cannabis and/or paraphernalia, photographs or videos of cannabis/cannabis paraphernalia and audio scripts describing cannabis use. Neutral cues were matched to cannabis cues and when identified included beach cues or office/school supplies. Three studies achieved quality scores less than 16, indicating some risk of bias.

Overall effect size

Tables 2 and 3 and Fig. 2 show the individual effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each outcome measure within each included study. Inclusion of the random slope for the two conditions improved model fit [AIC = 89.9 versus 88.1, likelihood ratio test (LRT) = 2.25, d.f. = 2, P = 0.32]. Analysis of overall effects (k = 47) indicated that participants showed reactivity to cannabis cues compared to baseline and to neutral cues. High heterogeneity of effect size was evident (see Fig. 2). Furthermore, the funnel plot and the rank correlation test demonstrated evidence of potential publication bias (see Fig. 3).

In order to investigate whether theoretically and methodologically relevant cue–reactivity variables moderated cannabis cue–reactivity, the overall meta-analysis was rerun with mean centred age, mean centred proportion of male participants, 24 hours of abstinence (yes/no), exposure presentation order (fixed, random, counterbalanced) and cue type (real ‘in-vivo’/representation) included as moderators. Being abstinent for less than 24 hours (d = −0.81, 95% CI = −1.90, 0.28), having more males in a study (d = 0.72, 95% CI = −1.88, 3.33) or presenting cues in a random order (d = 0.25, 95% CI = −0.48, 0.97) were not statistically significant moderators, and overall estimates only narrowly changed when these moderators were included in the model (see Fig. 2). Consequently, these moderators were not included in subgroup analyses.

Subgroup analyses: self-report measures

Analysis of Marijuana Craving Questionnaire (MCQ; k = 13) scores indicated that participants self-reported stronger cravings in response to cannabis cues compared to baseline and compared to neutral cues. Similarly, analysis of Visual Analogue Scale (VAS scores; k = 15) scores also indicated that participants self-reported stronger cravings in response to cannabis cues compared to baseline and neutral cues. While no heterogeneity of effect size was evident for MCQ scores, there was low heterogeneity for VAS scores (see Fig. 2). The funnel plot and the rank correlation test for both MCQ scores (τ = −0.03, P = 0.95) and VAS scores (τ = −0.07, P = 0.77) demonstrated no evidence of potential publication bias.

Subgroup analyses: psychophysiological measures

Analysis of peak electrodermal activity (EDA; k = 5) indicated that participants sweated more when exposed to cannabis cues compared to baseline and neutral cues. Analysis of peak heart rate (HR; k = 6) indicated that participants experienced higher HR when exposed to cannabis cues compared to baseline, but not to neutral cues. Lastly, analysis of Pz P300 amplitude (k = 4) indicated that participants had greater reactions along the midline of the scalp when exposed to cannabis cues compared to neutral cues. High heterogeneity of effect size was evident for all measures (see Fig. 2).

Narrative analysis

One included study examined changes in blood pressure and skin temperature after presentation of cannabis cues. That study found that skin temperature decreased in response to cannabis cues compared to a baseline condition (moderate effect) and a neutral cue condition (small effect). This study also found that blood pressure did not change in comparison to baseline, but that a small increase in peak systolic blood pressure occurred when participants were presented with cannabis cues compared with a neutral cue.

Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis for the overall model that excluded the three studies at a greater than low risk of bias 19, 21, 29 resulted in slight changes to the overall estimates, but the effects remained large (k = 34; baseline comparison: d = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.0.37, 1.27, P < 0.001; neutral cue comparison: d = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.39, 1.27, P < 0.001). The inclusion of random slopes improved model fit (AIC: 64.1 versus 65.0, LRT = 4.9, d.f. = 2, P = 0.085). High heterogeneity of effect size remained (I2 = 79.9%, Q = 159.14, P < 0.001). The rank correlation test did not suggest the presence of publication bias, but the funnel plot did (see Fig. 4). Examination of the funnel plot shows that asymmetry might have been due to three Pz P300 studies having larger effect sizes and variances relative to the other studies. Thus, a second sensitivity analysis was conducted that excluded the aforementioned studies and included Pz P300 as a moderator. Discussion

Overall, this meta-analysis found that regular cannabis users show moderate to large reactions in response to cannabis cues and that the magnitude of these reactions may not differ substantially as a function of comparison condition. This statement, however, must be qualified based on subgroup results. While the magnitude of effects was only slightly larger when comparisons were made to baseline conditions rather than neutral cue conditions for self-report measures, the magnitude of cue–reactivity was substantially larger when comparing cannabis cues to baseline conditions for peak EDA and peak HR. Unexpectedly, theoretically relevant moderators did not influence the estimates for the overall model. Conversely, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that controlling for Pz P300 effect sizes and removing studies at greater than low risk of bias resulted in a more robust overall model. The sensitivity analyses showed that studies at risk of bias and Pz P300 effects explained much of the overall model's heterogeneity and that, on average, cue–reactivity studies may produce moderate effect sizes, but that Pz P300 studies are associated with significantly larger effect sizes.

A previous meta-analysis that examined event-related potentials (ERPs) for drug cues (cocaine, nicotine, heroin, alcohol and cannabis) in comparison to neutral cues found that the P300 component at the parietal electrode site (Pz) was associated with moderate effects (g = 0.51) 31, but that the P300 amplitude for depressant drugs resulted only in a large effect size (g = 0.81). As the previous meta-analysis only included one cannabis cue–reactivity study, our combined findings suggest that cannabis stimuli may become more salient for cannabis users and may receive more attention (d = 2.32) than do cues for other types of drug users. This enhanced motivated attention for cannabis cues may contribute substantially to cannabis users’ inability to control their drug use 32. As prospective studies examining the causal role of attentional bias on cannabis use are lacking, future studies should examine the association between motivated attention for cannabis cues and cannabis use and relapse after treatment.

The current meta-analysis indicated that cannabis cue–reactivity paradigms have elicited quite variable physiological reactions depending on the type of outcome assessed. While the direction of effects is consistent with Carter & Tiffany's 6 meta-analysis examining other drugs of abuse, the variability noted in this meta-analysis is not consistent. In the previous meta-analysis, small effect sizes were found when examining heart rate (d = 0.26), EDA (d = 0.40) and skin temperature (d = −0.24) for alcohol, opiate, cocaine and nicotine cues in comparison to neutral cues. In the current meta-analysis, the cannabis cue versus neutral cue effect size for peak HR was trivial (d = 0.14), but moderate-to-large (d = 0.77) for peak EDA. The one study that examined temperature and blood pressure showed a small decrease (d = −0.30) in skin temperature and a small increase in systolic blood pressure (d = 0.34) when comparing cannabis cues to neutral cues 24.

The complexity of these findings argues against the stimulus-substitution theory of Pavlovian conditioning. If cannabis cues act as mere replacements for cannabis use, they should only elicit conditioned responses that mimic the acute effects of cannabis. Consistent with this idea, laboratory studies have shown that acute cannabis administration induces tachycardia reliably, can increase systolic blood pressure and might decrease skin temperature 33-35. However, laboratory research also has shown that sweating (EDA) is a feature of withdrawal 36. While it is possible that conditioned stimuli could elicit responses similar to both cannabis use and withdrawal at the same time, a more parsimonious explanation is that the responses elicited by cannabis cues are due to their affective evaluation. Stimuli evaluated as pleasant and arousing have been shown to increase heart rate and skin conductance, respectively 37. Thus, cannabis stimuli may obtain their properties through evaluative conditioning 38. Repetitive pairings between cannabis paraphernalia and cannabis use, a positive stimulus for cannabis users, might cause cannabis paraphernalia to be liked.

This conceptualization is consistent with the incentive sensitization theory of addiction 39. Incentive sensitization theory posits that the interaction between associative learning and neuroadaptions in the brain that occur due to repeated drug use cause drugs and drug stimuli to incur pathological levels of incentive salience. Stimuli with incentive salience can elicit wanting and liking for unconditioned rewards (getting high) as well as wanting and liking for the stimuli themselves (i.e. motivate individuals to approach them in their own right). Such wanting can be unconscious (physiological) or conscious (craving).

In the current meta-analysis, moderate effects were found for self-reported craving regardless of using a single- or a multi-item instrument. Interestingly, Carter & Tiffany 6 found large effects for self-reported craving for nicotine, opiates and cocaine cues when comparing them to neutral cues. These discrepant results suggest that cannabis users may experience less explicit craving, but greater unconscious responses, than other drug users.

Elaborated Intrusion Theory 4, 40 provides additional insights into discrepancies between unconscious and explicit results in both the current and past studies 6. Within that theory, conditioned physiological responses trigger craving only when they enter awareness and are linked to cannabis by internal attributional or associative processes, or because of a salient external cue. Cognitive or environmental cues can also trigger an intrusive craving cognition without a previous physiological response. The initial thought triggers both anticipatory pleasure or relief and increased awareness of physiological deprivation or dysphoria which, if sufficiently intense, triggers subsequent elaboration of related cognitions in working memory. This elaboration maintains or intensifies the affective or motivational power of the cognitions and also generates further associations and physiological responses. Thus, subjective craving is thought to require additional associative or controlled processes beyond a simple physiological response.

Limitations

A few issues deserve consideration. First, while funnel plots and rank correlation tests showed no evidence of publication bias once controlling for Pz P300 studies and excluding studies at risk of bias, VAS scores and the overall model showed more heterogeneity of effect sizes than expected by chance. This heterogeneity may be due to differences in the study protocols and methods used across studies rather than as a function of effect sizes. Alternatively, this variability of differences between studies may be due to some of the included studies having a small sample size and also by including a relatively small number of studies in the meta-analysis 41. This latter issue means that our non-significant results regarding theoretically relevant moderators may be the result of lower power and therefore treated with caution. Other meta-analyses examining cue–reactivity also have been fraught by small sample sizes 31, 42. This trend is due probably to the difficulty associated with recruiting drug users for research and for carrying out cue–reactivity studies in general. Lastly, lack of consistent reporting on drug consumption levels made it impossible to classify participants systematically across the studies and to examine use patterns as a moderator. Based on the limitations of this meta-analysis, researchers should focus upon devising new methods to increase research participation among cannabis users, as well as on reporting systematically on length of use, days of use and quantity of use among their study participants.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that regular cannabis users react to the presentation of cannabis cues. This reactivity may be both unconscious and conscious, and cannabis users may experience more unconscious reactivity than conscious reactivity compared to other drug users. Future studies would benefit from directly examining cue–reactivity among cannabis users and other drug users and from assessing if physiological responses or self-reported craving is more predictive of ongoing cannabis use. The current findings suggest that repetitive use may result in a much stronger attentional bias to cannabis cues than a conscious perception of craving for the drug. As implicit processes are theorized to motivate drug use, reducing the incentive salience of cannabis cues should be an important focus of future research. Repeated cue exposure might be helpful in this regard, as breaking the association between cannabis paraphernalia and drug-taking may not only reduce physiological responses elicited by the drug cues, but may also reduce how much an individual wants and likes the paraphernalia themselves 43.
