Abstract

Aims

To compare regional brain activation among adolescents with bipolar disorder and co-occurring cannabis use disorder.

Design

Cross-sectional study.

Setting

Cincinnati, OH, USA.

Participants

Adolescents with bipolar disorder (BP, n = 14), adolescents with cannabis use disorder (MJ, n = 13), adolescents with co-occurring cannabis use and bipolar disorders (BPMJ, n = 25) and healthy adolescents (HC, n = 15).

Measurements

Cannabis craving, substance use, Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent (BOLD) signal assessed by the Marijuana Craving Questionnaire (MCQ), Teen-Addiction Severity Index (T-ASI) and a cannabis cue–reactivity task during a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) session, respectively.

Findings

The BP group exhibited significantly greater brain activation than the BPMJ group in the right amygdala (F = 4.14, P = 0.046), left nucleus accumbens (F = 3.8, P = 0.02), left thalamus (F = 3.8, P < 0.05) and the right thalamus (F = 6.2, P = 0.02). The BP group exhibited significantly greater activation than the HC group in the left nucleus accumbens (F = 11.5, P = 0.0001), right thalamus (F = 4.9, P = 0.03) and the left striatum (F = 3.6, P = 0.04). Left amygdala activation of the BPMJ group trended towards being significantly negatively correlated with the number of joints smoked (R = −0.4, P = 0.06).

Conclusions

Bipolar adolescents with comorbid cannabis use do not exhibit the same over-activation of the regions involved in emotional processing as seen in adolescents with bipolar disorder alone. The absence of these findings in patients with comorbid bipolar and cannabis use disorders suggests that these individuals may have a unique endophenotype of bipolar disorder or that cannabis use may alter brain activation uniquely in bipolar disorder patients who use cannabis.

Introduction

Epidemiological data suggest that up to 60% of individuals with bipolar disorder have a comorbid substance use disorder 1. Specifically, adolescents with bipolar disorder most frequently use cannabis 2, and those with co-occurring bipolar and cannabis use disorders have a worse outcome and poorer treatment adherence than those with either illness alone. These individuals exhibit greater neurostructural abnormalities in frontal and temporal cortical regions, as well as in subcortical areas linked with emotion and motivational regulation, when compared with adolescents diagnosed with bipolar disorder alone 3.

In brain pathways associated with reward, craving and addiction, dopamine is released into the nucleus accumbens and the pre-frontal cortex as the mesolimbic pathway and the mesocortical pathway, respectively 4. Dopamine neuron cell bodies, found in the substantia nigra, manufacture and release dopamine into the striatum as the nigrostriatal pathway 5. Indeed, functional neuroimaging studies have shown that adult cannabis users exhibit increased activation in brain regions of the reward pathway during a cannabis cue–reactivity task 6. Additionally, studies have shown that young adult cannabis users exhibited increased activation in their inferior and middle frontal gyri 7, and failed to deactivate their right hippocampus during a visuospatial working memory task 8.

Neuroimaging studies have shown that adolescents with bipolar disorder demonstrate abnormal activation in their pre-frontal regions, insula, amygdala, parahippocampal regions, dorsolateral and ventral lateral pre-frontal cortices, anterior cingulate, fusiform gyrus and thalamus 9-12. However, there have been no studies addressing neural function in youth with co-occurring bipolar and cannabis use disorders. Given the high rates of co-occurrence, such studies might clarify how cannabis use impacts bipolar neurophysiology, perhaps improving our understanding of the relationship between these disorders, and leading to more effective treatment options.

With these considerations in mind, the aim of our study was to compare brain activation in the regions hypothesized to be associated with the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder among individuals with comorbid bipolar and substance use disorders to those with either illness alone during a cannabis cue–reactivity task among four groups: adolescents with co-occurring bipolar and cannabis use disorders, adolescents with bipolar disorder who do not use cannabis, adolescents with a cannabis use disorder and healthy adolescents. Based on findings from previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies, we hypothesized that adolescents with comorbid bipolar and cannabis use disorders would exhibit greater dysfunctional activation in brain regions associated with reward (nucleus accumbens and striatum), emotional processing (thalamus and amygdala) and inhibitory pre-frontal regions (Brodmann areas 10, 11 and 47) when compared to adolescents with bipolar disorder or a cannabis use disorder, alone 6-8, 13.

Methods

Subjects

Adolescents and young adults, aged 12–21 years, were recruited from the University of Cincinnati and Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center into one of four groups: those with comorbid bipolar and a cannabis use disorder (BPMJ, n = 25); those with bipolar disorder and without a cannabis use disorder (BP, n = 14); those with a cannabis use disorder without bipolar disorder (MJ, n = 13); and healthy subjects without any DSM-IV-TR Axis I disorder (HC, n = 15). All participants were unmedicated and subjects were group-matched for age, race and sex (Table 1). This cohort was published previously in a spectroscopy analysis in the Journal of Dual Diagnosis 14. This study was conducted as a part of a treatment protocol by the National Institute of Drug Abuse (NIDA).

DSM-IV-TR diagnoses were confirmed by trained raters (kappa > 0.9) who administered the Washington University in St Louis Kiddie Schedule of Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (WASH-U KSADS) for Axis I disorders other than substance use disorders and the Semi-Structured Assessment for the Genetics of Alcoholism, Adolescent version (SSAGA-A) for substance use disorders 15-17. Participants were excluded for any of the following: IQ ≤ 70 on the Wechsler Abbreviated Scales of Intelligence, any unstable medical or neurological illness as determined by a study physician, a positive urine pregnancy test, any contraindication to undergoing an MRI scan (e.g. braces or claustrophobia), a Tanner Score < 3, current use of a psychotropic medication or any history of significant head trauma resulting in a loss of consciousness for greater than 5 minutes.

All study participants under the age of 18 provided written assent and their legal guardians provided written informed consent. All study participants over the age of 18 provided written consent after study procedures were fully explained. This study was approved by the University of Cincinnati Institutional Review Board and information collected was covered under a certificate of confidentiality issued by NIH.

Symptom assessments

The Marijuana Craving Questionnaire (MCQ) 18 was administered to all subjects immediately before and after their MRI scanning session to assess whether craving was induced by the pictures presented during the fMRI task. Subjects who demonstrated ≥20% increase from baseline in total MCQ scores were closely monitored until their craving scores returned to within 20% of their baseline score. The MCQ is a 17-item self-rated instrument that assesses craving to smoke marijuana. Items are rated on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Scoring of the MCQ permits assessment of four factors (subscales) that are relevant to marijuana craving. These factors include: compulsivity, an inability to control marijuana use; emotionality, use of marijuana in anticipation of relief from withdrawal or negative mood; expectancy, anticipation of positive outcomes from smoking marijuana; and purposefulness, intention and planning to use marijuana for positive outcomes. Reliability and validity estimates have been established for these subscales as well as a general (overall) factor 18.

The Teen-Addiction Severity Index (T-ASI) was also administered to all subjects 19, 20. The T-ASI is a semi-structured interview developed as a standardized instrument for periodic evaluation of adolescent substance use. The T-ASI is an age-appropriate modification of the Addiction Severity Index. It yields 70 ratings in seven domains: substance use, school status, employment/support status, family relations, peer/social relationships, legal status and psychiatric status. It has established validity and inter-rater reliability.

The Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) and the Children's Depression Rating Scale-Revised version (CDRS-R) were administered by raters with established high inter-rater reliability (ICC > 0.90) to evaluate manic and depressed symptoms, respectively 21-23.

fMRI procedures

During their fMRI session study participants performed a cue–reactivity task, which consisted of pictures of cannabis-related cues as well as neutral pictures. The neutral pictures originated from the International Affective Picture System (University of Florida, Gainesville, Florida) and the cannabis pictures were collected from public sources. The cue–reactivity task comprised 10 epochs with 30 seconds per epoch. Neutral pictures were presented during the first five epochs and cannabis pictures were presented during the second five epochs (Fig. 1). The images were presented in five blocks of six images. Every image was displayed for 4750 ms with a 250 ms inter-stimulus delay. Between each block, there was a 20-second rest period. During the rest period, a blinking cross-hair was presented on the screen, serving as a fixation point. The task was programmed in E-Prime (http://pstnet.com). Intermittently, a red number ‘1’ would appear at the top of one picture. Subjects pressed a button box when a red number ‘1’ appeared at the top of the stimulus to ensure that they were actively viewing the task. The percentage correct was calculated and reaction time recorded during the neutral and cannabis stimuli, separately. This task was piloted both in and out of the scanner to ensure a good distribution of scores and an association with increased craving on the Marijuana Craving Questionnaire (MCQ) using pre-task and post-task scores.

All scans were completed at the University of Cincinnati's Center for Imaging Research (UC-CIR) using a 4-Tesla Varian Unity INOVA Whole Body MRI system (Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a head TEM coil (MR Instruments Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). Subjects completed the fMRI session in which whole-brain images (volumes) were acquired every 2 seconds, while performing the task using a T2-weighted gradient-echo echoplanar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence. Visual stimuli were presented using high-resolution video goggles (Resonance Technologies, Inc., Northridge, CA, USA). To minimize subject motion during scanning procedures, all subjects were instructed to remain still and foam packing was inserted around their head.

MR data processing and analyses

The UC-CIR MRI raw free induction decay (FID) data were reconstructed using in-house software developed in IDL (Interactive Data Language), which converts raw FID files into AFNI format (Analysis of Functional NeuroImages; http://afni.nimh.nih.gov/afni). Each MDEFT image was reformatted into the same three-dimensional (3-D) space, which allowed for consistent viewing of the region of interest (ROI) boundaries between subjects. Structural and echo-planar (functional) images were co-registered based upon scanner coordinates. Additionally, each volume was inspected for signal artifacts using a semi-automated algorithm in AFNI and excluded from further analysis if uncorrectable head movement occurred. Anatomical and functional maps were transformed into stereotactic Talairach space using the International Consortium for Brain Mapping 452 template (Laboratory of NeuroImaging, University of California–Los Angeles, Los Angeles, California). Following normalization, brain activation was calculated in AFNI using an algorithm (3dDeconvolve) that compares the actual hemodynamic response to a canonical hemodynamic response function.

A hypothesis-driven ROI mask was created a priori for the following regions of interest based upon our hypotheses (Fig. 2): ventral pre-frontal area (Brodmann areas 10, 11 and 47), amygdala, thalamus, nucleus accumbens and striatum 24. The boundaries of these ROIs were based on previous reports and Talairach coordinates 25. The activation [measured using the Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent (BOLD) signal] was determined by taking the average of the whole anatomically defined region of interest (excluding zero-activated voxels) for each subject. Values were obtained within ROIs when the subjects were looking at cannabis pictures and neutral pictures. The average percent signal change of the BOLD signal, from the ROI, was then used for all analyses. A voxelwise approach was not completed for this analysis.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, 2002–2008). Analyses of variance (ANOVA) and χ2 tests were used to examine group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics. Change in Marijuana Craving Questionnaire (MCQ) scores (post-scan score minus pre-scan score) were evaluated using PROC UNIVARIATE, to determine the validity of the task by examining the change in the groups that used cannabis (BPMJ and MJ) and the groups that did not use cannabis (HC and BP). A two (BP groups) × two (MJ groups) ranked non-parametric factorial design was used to determine group effects in brain activation during the cannabis and neutral pictures.

Post-hoc analyses examining individual group comparisons were performed when the BP × MJ interaction term was statistically significant. Additionally, Spearman's correlations between the change in (1) MCQ subscales (post-scan minus pre-scan) and general factor scores and (2) general factor scores and ROI activations were completed. These correlations were evaluated to assess whether change in aspects of craving were associated with specific ROI activations during cannabis stimuli. Spearman's correlations, to examine associations between the number of joints smoked in the past month and ROI activation, were performed in the MJ and BPMJ groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant for all tests.

Results

Clinical characteristics

There were no statistically significant group differences in age, sex or race (Table 1). Statistically significant differences in IQ among groups were observed (P < 0.0001, HC > MJ > BP > BPMJ; Table 1). Therefore, IQ was used as a covariate in all analyses. Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences among groups in the percentage correct of task performance; however, statistically significant group differences were seen in reaction time (P = 0.03, BPMJ > BP > HC = MJ; Table 1).

Craving assessments

MCQ compulsivity and general factor scores significantly increased in the BPMJ and the MJ (cannabis-using) groups following the cue–reactivity task (P = 0.008 and P = 0.03, respectively). The HC and BP group scores remained the same for all subscales as well as general factor scores.

There were no significant group effects while viewing the cannabis pictures (Table 2).

fMRI activation and cannabis craving and use

Spearman's correlations revealed that, while viewing cannabis pictures, the change in general factor scores were negatively correlated with nucleus accumbens activation in the BP group (R = −0.75, P = 0.002). Left amygdala activation of the BPMJ group trended towards being significantly negatively correlated with the number of joints smoked (R = −0.4, P = 0.06; Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study suggests that bipolar patients without cannabis use exhibit dysfunction of limbic regions involved in emotional regulation compared to adolescents with comorbid bipolar disorder and cannabis use disorder. Specifically, adolescents with bipolar disorder exhibited significantly increased brain activation when compared to adolescents with bipolar disorder and comorbid cannabis use disorder in regions involved in emotional processing (right and left thalamus, right amygdala) and a region associated with reward (left nucleus accumbens). Consistent with previous studies, these findings reveal that bipolar adolescents have overactive limbic systems and this greater activation may be a result of dysfunctional pre-frontal–amygdala inhibitory pathways 26, 27. Contrary to our hypothesis, these findings were not seen in adolescents with comorbid bipolar and cannabis use disorders.

The lack of increased activation of the brain regions associated with emotional processing and reward observed in the BPMJ group may suggest that this group of adolescents has a different underlying neuropathology compared to adolescents with bipolar disorder and/or cannabis use alone. Alternatively, this lack of increased activation, as seen when these illnesses occur alone, may result from cannabis use itself. The trend towards a significant association between cannabis use and decreased amygdala activation found in the BPMJ cohort supports a direct effect of cannabis in these regions. Furthermore, this hypothesis of cannabis use is supported by other studies that found attenuated brain activity in task-activated regions in chronic cannabis users 28. One study, during a fearful faces task, found that cannabidiol (CBD) decreased the activation in the amygdala and anterior and posterior cingulate cortex of adult men with no personal or family history of a psychiatric disorder 29. A different study found that cannabinoids provoke psychosis/schizophrenia reflecting a mechanism common to neuroprotection: the reduction of NMDAR activity, which leads to glutamatergic hypofunction 30.

Several possible explanations exist for this mechanism of hypofunction. A study by Schacht et al. found that the amygdala and hippocampus volumes of heavy cannabis users reported smaller volumes in a particular genetic variation of a single nucleotide polymorphism in the cannabis receptor-1 gene responsible for cue-elicited craving and parahippocampal activation to cannabis cues 31. An fMRI study of patients with schizophrenia and comorbid cannabis use disorder found that the functioning of the medial pre-frontal cortex, thought to be impaired in patients with prominent negative symptoms, was more preserved in patients with a dual diagnosis 32. Additionally, a preliminary study of fMRI among cannabis users during performance of a virtual water maze task found that cannabis users exhibited a significantly lower fMRI BOLD signal in their right parahippocampal gyrus and cingulate gyrus than the non-cannabis users during a memory task 33. The authors hypothesize that this difference in activation is due to the difference in efficient utilization of neuronal resources between those who use cannabis and those who do not. Therefore, whether the reported findings of significantly lower brain activation in the limbic regions of the BPMJ group, when compared to adolescents with bipolar disorder, are due to an originally smaller amygdala predisposed by a specific genetic variant, preservation of function (lack of overactive limbic regions as seen in the BP group) or difference in efficient utilization of neuronal resources by cannabis use still needs to be determined.

Additionally, contrary to our hypothesis, there were no significant BP × MJ associations found when the subjects were viewing cannabis pictures. The MCQ scores demonstrated an increase in craving after viewing these images in the cannabis-using groups, which may have potentially caused an emotional response in the BPMJ group, the group familiar with the images, leading to an increase in brain activation in their limbic regions and, thus, resulting in no differences observed between the BP and BPMJ groups. This emotional response did not occur when the BPMJ groups were viewing neutral pictures.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of our study. First, the number of subjects is small and the participants included adolescents, aged 12–21 years, who are at widely varying neurodevelopmental stages. Secondly, this study is cross-sectional, and conclusions cannot be drawn regarding causation. Thirdly, we did not control for the length of time since each adolescent's last cannabis use and his or her total amount of cannabis used; nevertheless, this information is included in Table 1. Additionally, quantifying the frequency and intensity of cannabis use can be challenging; however, in order to be consistent across participants, we converted the amount of cannabis use reported to the equivalent number of joints smoked during the past month. This may not be the most accurate unit of measurement, but as per previously published data 34, as well as the recommendation of our adolescent substance use consultants, a priori, we proposed to use the joint as our standard unit of measurement. Next, we did not control for alcohol use and no other substance use was permitted. However, subjects were not scanned if currently intoxicated with cannabis or any other substance. This was determined by self-report, time-line follow-back interview and a breathalyzer test. Finally, although not statistically significant, there were differences in demographic characteristics, including gender, which may have contributed to the findings.

Despite these limitations, to our knowledge this study is the first to examine the effects of cannabis use in adolescents with bipolar disorder. Future studies to determine whether the differences in activation between adolescents with bipolar disorder who use cannabis and those who do not represent underlying differences in brain dysfunction or are a result from cannabis use are needed. Additionally, future neuroimaging research in gene × environment interactions 35 may help in the understanding of underlying mechanisms for the high rate of comorbidity between bipolar and cannabis use disorders. Finally, investigation into the effects of cannabis use on healthy comparison adolescents, to understand more clearly the direct effects of cannabis use, are needed.
