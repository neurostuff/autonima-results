ABSTRACT

Aims  During the development of drug addiction, initial hedonic effects decrease when substance use becomes habitual and ultimately compulsive. Animal research suggests that these changes are represented by a transition from prefrontal cortical control to subcortical striatal control and within the striatum from ventral to dorsal domains of the striatum, but only limited evidence exists in humans. In this study we address this hypothesis in the context of alcohol dependence.

Design, setting and participants  Non-abstinent heavy social drinkers (n = 21, 5.0 ± 1.5 drinks/day, 13 of them were alcohol-dependent according to DSM-IV) and light social drinkers (n = 10, 0.4 ± 0.4 drinks/day) were examined.

Measurements  We used a cue-reactivity functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) design during which pictures of alcoholic beverages and neutral control stimuli were presented.

Findings  In the dorsal striatum heavy drinkers showed significant higher activations compared to light drinkers, whereas light social drinkers showed higher cue-induced fMRI activations in the ventral striatum and in prefrontal areas compared to heavy social drinkers [region of interest analyses, P < 0.05 false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected]. Correspondingly, ventral striatal activation in heavy drinkers correlated negatively with obsessive-compulsive craving, and furthermore we found a positive association between cue-induced activation in the dorsal striatum and obsessive-compulsive craving in all participants.

Conclusions  In line with our hypothesis we found higher cue-induced activation of the ventral striatum in social compared to heavy drinkers, and higher dorsal striatal activation in heavy drinkers. Increased prefrontal activation may indicate that social drinkers activate cortical control when viewing alcohol cues, which may prevent the development of heavy drinking or alcohol dependence. Our results suggest differentiating treatment research depending on whether alcohol use is hedonic or compulsive.

INTRODUCTION

During the development of drug addiction, initial hedonic effects decrease when substance use becomes habitual and ultimately compulsive [1]. In terms of the ‘incentive salience theory of addiction’[2], behaviour is guided by initial ‘liking’ and subsequently by ‘wanting’, and in an expansion of this theory [1] by a subjective state of ‘must do’. Everitt & Robbins [1] hypothesized that at the neural level these changes are represented by a transition of neural processing from prefrontal cortical to striatal control and within the striatum from ventral to dorsal domains of the striatum. They further described that both Pavlovian and instrumental conditioning were essential for the maintenance of addiction. In Pavlovian conditioning formerly neutral environmental stimuli become associated with substance use and become conditioned stimuli (CS). By linking these CS with the reinforcing effect of drugs, presentation of CS alone can act as reinforcer thereby enhancing the probability of drug-seeking and -taking behaviour (Pavlovian-instrumental transfer). This might explain why several human neuroimaging studies observed activation of the mesocorticolimbic reward system during the presentation of drug-associated stimuli (e.g. pictures, taste).

On the neural level, ‘wanting’ might be mediated by the ventral striatum, and habit learning and the state of ‘must do’ by the dorsal striatum. Evidence from animal studies supports this view: once substance use becomes compulsive, CS evoked dopamine release in the dorsal striatum [3], and cocaine-seeking behaviour could only be limited by dopamine receptor blockade in the dorsal striatum, but not in the ventral striatum [4].

Human neuroimaging studies in recently detoxified addicts, which used drug-associated stimuli as CS, reported increased activation of the dorsal striatum as frequently as of the ventral striatum, for cocaine [5,6], alcohol [7,8], heroin [9] and nicotine dependence [10].

The main aim of this study was to assess systematically the following hypotheses in the context of alcohol dependence with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). (i) During initial alcohol use (i.e. in light social drinking), conditioned stimuli are processed in the ventral striatum, whereas in heavy use they are evaluated in the dorsal striatum, and that in parallel (ii) neuronal cue-reactivity shifts from prefrontal to striatal processing when comparing non-dependent light use to heavy and dependent drug use.

METHOD

Participants

Twenty-one non-abstinent heavy drinkers [nine female, 12 male, 5.0 ± 1.5 drinks/day (mean ± standard deviation, SD), range 3–9, one drink = 14 g alcohol, age 49 ± 11 years, abstinent for 1.2 ± 0.9 days] and 10 light social alcohol drinkers [three female, seven male, 0.4 ± 0.4 drinks/day, range: 0–1, age 44 ± 8 years, abstinent for 5.3 ± 6.1 days (one outlier with 199 days of abstinence discarded)] were included in the study. Volunteers were recruited through announcements in a local newspaper. None of them was treatment seeking. Thirteen of the heavy social drinkers had already developed alcohol dependence according to ICD-10 and DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. We aimed to cover a broad range of heavy drinkers from non-dependent to dependent drinkers, which is the reason for the larger sample size in heavy drinkers (compared to light social drinkers). Additionally, a questionnaire of obsessive-compulsive alcohol-related thoughts and behaviour was completed by all participants [Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS), German version [11]]. Before the fMRI scan, breath alcohol was zero, and none of the participants showed clinically relevant withdrawal symptoms, measured with the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol–Revised (CIW-Ar) [12] score (light drinkers 0.7 ± 0.8, range 0–2, heavy drinkers 0.6 ± 1.2, range 0–4). Exclusion criteria were psychotropic medication, drug use other than alcohol and tobacco (urine drug screen), any other severe illnesses, cerebral trauma and usual exclusion criteria for MRI. Handedness was assessed via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [13]. Only participants with a laterality quotient greater than 50 and a sufficient visual acuity [binocular (corrected) ≥ 0.8] were included. All participants provided informed written consent according to the declaration of Helsinki and the study was approved by the local ethics committee.

fMRI task

Sixty-five alcohol-related (categories beer, wine and spirits) and 40 neutral stimuli were presented in a blocked design with 13 blocks of alcohol stimuli and eight blocks of neutral stimuli. One block consisted of five stimuli, each presented for 4 sec, resulting in a total duration of 20 sec per block. Examples of the stimuli presented during the fMRI scan are provided in Fig. 1. After each block participants were asked to indicate their subjective craving for alcohol on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 (‘no craving at all’) to 100 ('severe craving’). Participants had to rate their subjective craving in a maximum period of 10 sec. Thereafter a black fixation cross was presented on a white background for a minimum of 10 sec. The total task duration was 12 minutes.

Alcohol-related pictures were taken from an own alcohol picture series, which has been shown to evoke subjective craving in detoxified alcohol-dependent patients (unpublished data). Neutral control cues were taken from the International Affective Picture Series (IAPS) [14] and were matched for colour distribution and complexity to the alcohol cues. We decided not to use pictures of non-alcoholic beverages as neutral control cues because pictures of non-alcoholic beverages could be perceived misleadingly as alcoholic beverages (e.g. a glass of water could be perceived as a glass of vodka). The order of the categories alcohol and neutral was pseudo-randomized, whereas the order of the pictures was randomized for each subject.

fMRI acquisition

Scanning was performed with a 3 T whole-body tomograph (MAGNETOM Trio with TIM technology; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). T2* weighted echo-planar images were acquired in a transversal orientation 30° clockwise to anterior commisures–posterior commisures (AC–PC)-line [repetition time (TR) = 2.41 sec, echo time (TE) = 25 ms, flip angle = 80°, 42 slices, slice thickness 2 mm, 1-mm gap, voxel dimensions 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, field of view (FOV) 192 × 192 mm2, 64 × 64 in-plane resolution] covering the whole brain. This short TE and the 30° flip to AC–PC orientation were chosen to minimize susceptibility artefacts. A total of 305 images were measured for each subject. In addition, a T1-weighted three-dimensional MPRAGE data set consisting of 192 sagittal slices [slice thickness 1 mm, 1 × 1 × 1 mm voxel size, FOV 256 × 256 mm2, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 3.03 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°] was acquired.

Images were presented via goggles using MRI Audio/Video Systems (Resonance Technology Inc., Los Angeles, CA, USA). Task presentation and recording of the behavioural responses was performed using Presentation® software (version 9.9; Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Albany, CA, USA).

fMRI pre-processing

Pre-processing and statistical analyses of brain imaging data were performed using SPM5 (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK). The first five scans were excluded from the analyses to avoid artefacts due to magnetic saturation effects. The remaining 300 scans were realigned spatially to correct for head motion over the course of the session and then normalized to an MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute, Quebec, Canada) echo-planar imaging (EPI) template. Subsequent smoothing was performed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel for group analysis [8 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM)].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the pre-processed fMRI data on the first (individual) level were performed by modelling the different conditions (beer versus wine versus spirits versus neutral stimuli; boxcar functions convoluted with the haemodynamic response function) as explanatory variables within the context of the general linear model (GLM) on a voxel-by-voxel basis with SPM5. We calculated an image for the contrast ‘favourite drinks versus neutral’ for each participant [e.g (1 0 0 –1) for beer drinkers and (1 1 0 –2) for beer and wine drinkers, etc]. Individual contrast images of the participants were included in second-level analyses to identify brain regions with a main effect of drinking state (independent sample t-test) and the association with OCDS score (correlation analysis). The threshold was set to P < 0.001 (uncorrected) for whole-brain analyses and P < 0.05 small volume corrected (SVC) for regions of interest (ROI), which were the ventral and dorsal striatum and the prefrontal cortex. For correlation analyses we extracted the mean parameter estimates from the individual contrast images for the ventral and dorsal striatum as ROIs (left, right and bilateral). The ROI mask for the ventral striatum was downloaded from the Nielsen & Hansen database [15] and converted to a binary mask with a threshold of ≥0.25. For the dorsal striatum we have created an own mask. With the extracted parameter estimates we conducted correlation analyses in SPSS (Statistical Package of the Social Sciences, version 16.0.2; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to examine the association between BOLD signal change and OCDS score.

RESULTS

OCDS scores differed significantly between the two groups [t = 6.241, P < 0.001; mean ± SD heavy drinkers: 11.5 ± 4.7 (range: 5–21) and light drinkers: 1.8 ± 1.8 (range: 0–5)].

The fMRI cue-reactivity paradigm activated a wide-area network of regions associated with the brain's reward system as well as attention and object recognition networks, similar to our previous cue-reactivity studies [7,8,16]. Whole-brain analyses showed increased activation of the left fusiform gyrus [(x,y,z) = (−36,–44,–14), t = 4.04, P < 0.001 uncorrected] and the left dorsal striatum [(x,y,z) = (−14,6,24), t = 4.17, P < 0.001 uncorrected] in heavy drinkers during the presentation of alcohol cues. Furthermore, we found a negative correlation between OCDS scores and right ventral striatum activation in the heavy drinking participants (Pearson's r = −0.402, P = 0.035, Fig. 4a). In the whole participant group (also including the light social drinkers) we only found a trend towards a negative correlation between ventral striatal activation and OCDS score (r = −0.291, P = 0.56 for the right ventral striatum and r = −0.280, P = 0.64 for bilateral ventral striatum). Furthermore, OCDS score was associated positively with dorsal striatum activation in the whole participant group (right dorsal striatum: r = 0.417, P = 0.010, Fig. 4b, bilateral dorsal striatum r = 0.344, P = 0.029).

DISCUSSION

Higher alcohol cue-induced activation of the dorsal striatum in 21 heavy drinkers, of whom 13 participants already had developed alcohol dependence, in comparison to light social drinkers is in accordance with the hypothesis of a shift of learning processes from ventral to dorsal domains of the striatum when the alcohol dependence proceeds [1]. The positive association between OCDS and dorsal striatal activation as well as the linear negative correlation of OCDS and ventral striatum activation additionally supports the decreasing relevance of cue processing in the ventral striatum when alcohol use becomes more obsessive and compulsive [1]. These correlations indicate that dorsal striatum activation is lowest in participants with low OCDS scores, whereas ventral striatum activation is highest in these individuals, and vice-versa. Hypotheses generating the experiments of Everitt & Robbins [1] were based upon animal studies using amphetamine or cocaine, which increase dopaminergic neurotransmission directly in the nucleus accumbens core. In these animal studies amphetamine or cocaine intake was paired with environmental stimuli associated closely in time and space with the effects of self-administered drugs. Thereby these environmental stimuli became conditioned stimuli (CS). The drug-paired CSs elicited a reinforcing dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens core [17]. Extended training of pairing CSs with reinforcing drug effects led to the development of habits, and subsequently drug-paired CSs evoked dopamine release in the dorsal striatum and not in the nucleus accumbens (i.e. ventral striatum) in microdialysis studies in rats [3]. Consistently, the dopamine receptor antagonist flupenthixol reduced well-established cocaine-seeking when administered into the dorsal striatum, but had no effect when infused in the nucleus accumbens core [18,19]. Everitt & Robbins also suggested validating their findings in other classes of addictive substances [20]. In our translational approach we have transferred successfully their pre-clinical hypothesis to humans, and shown their validity for a different substance class, namely alcohol, which impacts only indirectly on dopamine functioning. The shift of processing from the ventral to the dorsal striatum with enhanced importance of compulsive substance use might therefore be a general feature of late substance dependence.

According to the hypothesis of a shift of learning processes from ventral to dorsal domains of the striatum with progression of the alcohol dependence [1], we have found a decrease in brain activity in the ventral striatum in heavy drinkers. The degree of obsessive-compulsive alcohol thoughts and behaviour, which was the key feature of Everitt & Robbins’ hypothesis [1], may even be covered better by the OCDS than by the diagnostic criteria of alcohol dependence (ICD-10 and DSM-IV criteria also include items that are independent of obsessive-compulsive alcohol-related thoughts and behaviour, e.g. withdrawal symptoms or tolerance), indicated by the positive/negative correlations between brain activation in the dorsal/ventral striatum and OCDS score.

Prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex was found to be involved in the adaptive processing of incentive stimuli and was shown to influence the selection and execution of reward-directed behavioural responses [16]. Increased activation of the prefrontal cortex of light social drinkers compared to heavy-drinking participants during the presentation of alcohol cues are in line with Everitt & Robbins’ hypothesis of a transition of neural processing from prefrontal cortical to striatal control with progression of the dependence [1]. This may indicate that light social drinkers are able to acquire cortical control over striatal drug-seeking effects while watching alcohol cues to prevent loss of control over alcohol intake, which may prevent the development of heavy drinking or alcohol dependence. In contrast, heavy drinkers may not be able to acquire cortical control over behaviour when alcohol cues are presented. Another possibility could be a loss of cortical control in heavy drinkers in their ‘early stage of dependence’. Unfortunately, with our cross-sectional design it is not possible to prove one of these two interpretations.

Recent studies have shown that activation of the pre-frontal cortex during fMRI cue-exposure was found mainly in non-treatment-seeking participants [16]. None of our participants was treatment-seeking. Therefore one can assume that increased activation of the prefrontal cortex in light social drinkers compared to heavy drinkers is not a result of treatment-seeking.

Previous human fMRI cue-reactivity studies in alcohol use disorders

A literature review revealed a total of 10 alcohol-cue fMRI studies in alcohol-dependent patients [7,8,21–28] and three studies in heavy non-dependent drinkers [29–31]; see Table 2. Of the 10 studies in alcohol-dependent patients, five failed to observe cue-induced activation of the striatum; three reported increased activation of the ventral striatum and two of the dorsal striatum. Of the three alcohol-cue fMRI studies in heavy non-dependent drinkers, one was performed in adolescents and did not find striatal activation [30], the second reported activation of the dorsal striatum but not the ventral striatum [29] and the third restricted the analysis to the ventral striatum, in which they found increased activation to alcohol cues [31]. The latter study may be biased, as it focused exclusively upon the ventral striatum, thereby neglecting activations in the dorsal striatum. The findings of our study suggest adding the dorsal striatum as a region of interest in future drug- or alcohol-cue fMRI studies [8,32–42].

Clinical implications

Everitt & Robbins’ hypothesis may be especially helpful to explain why several attempts to block rewarding effects of drugs directly by dopamine D2 blockers or the mu-opiate antagonist naltrexone have been successful only in a subgroup of alcohol-dependent patients: these medications may show an effect only in non-dependent heavy alcohol users or ‘early’ alcohol-dependent patients [43], when mood-enhancing and reinforcing effects in the ventral striatum play a major role for alcohol consumption. Later alcohol dependence may better be conceptualized as a disorder with obsessive-compulsive features and altered stress sensitivity [43].

Limitations

A potential limitation of our study is the small sample size in the group of light social drinkers. Therefore replication of our results is important in larger samples. This would also increase statistical power. Longitudinal studies are required to examine further the hypothesis of a transition of cue-processing from ventral to dorsal domains of the striatum during the development of addiction.
